\chapter{Tools of Radio Astronomy}

Radio astronomy found its beginning at Bell labs, Karl Janksy announced in 1933 the discovery radio emission coming from the Milky Way when observing the atmosphere at 20~MHz \citep{jansky_radio_1933}. This was soon improved upon by Grote Reber, who built a radio telescope in his backyard and made deliberate observations of many radio bright objects, publishing them in astronomical journals. Radio transmitters and receivers made great strides during the war years due to the development of radar equipment for military purposes. In the following years many who had developed these technologies, turned to study the `noise' that was picked up from extraterrestrial sources, forming the foundation of modern radio astronomy. 

The radio window from the Earth's surface extends roughly from 30~MHz $(\lambda\sim10~\mathrm{m})$ up to about 1300~GHz $(\lambda\sim0.3~\mathrm{mm})$ as shown in \cref{fig:chpt2radiowindow}. The lower limit of this window arises from adsorption of radio waves by free electrons in the ionosphere. While radio waves can technically be observed down to 4.5~MHz if the frequency of the radio waves is below that of the plasma frequency, $\nu_p$ the adsorption becomes significantly more prominent such that

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\nu_P}{\mathrm{kHz}} = 8.97 \sqrt{\dfrac{N_e}{\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}}, 
\end{equation}

Where $N_e$ is the electron density of the plasma. There is also other contributions that effect this, such as solar activity which make this limit variable. This means that to study kHz frequencies or an unrefracted view of $\lesssim 30~\mathrm{MHz}$ the radio sky must be observed from space. The upper radio limit isn't observed at any single wavelength, but considerations become important at mm wavelengths as resonant absorption comes into effect from the lowest rotation band of molecules in the troposphere \citep{WilsonToolsofRadioastron}. This is mainly attributed to water vapour $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$, adsorption also occurs at higher frequencies, but here the atmosphere is opaque to radio waves. Around 300~GHz is considered to the upper limit in a radio context where atmospheric absorption at sea level becomes very strong, with additional losses from clouds and rain. The work in this thesis is carried out from $0.1-4000~\mathrm{MHz}$. 

This chapter discusses some fundamentals of radio telescopes and the processes that go into generating science useable products used to explore the objects discussed in \cref{chap:introduction}.

\section{From Photon to Filterbank} \label{sec:chap2photon2fil}

Radio telescopes can often be seen as conceptually complex but share much common ground with contemporary optical telescopes. An antenna is often thought of as a `single pixel' in a charged couple device (CCD) with an array of antennas forming an array of pixels that can be used to observe the sky. There are also parallels to be drawn between the mirrors in optical telescope and the parabolic dishes seen on radio telescopes which will later be discussed. This section aims to provide enough theoretical context to understand how an incident photon on a radio telescope gets turned into science useable data products such as filterbanks. 

For any electromagnetic wave coming from an astronomical source the wave is considered to be in the far-field, and the wavefronts are therefore locally planar across the extent of a radio telescope. Radio telescopes sample the electric field associated with these incoming plane waves. The general time-dependent electric field vector may be expressed as,

\begin{equation}
    \textbf{E} (t,r) = (\hat a E_a + \hat b E_b) \ \exp(2 \pi i (ft-kr))
    \label{eq:wave_equation}
\end{equation}

%TODO: talk about r_1, r_2, phase difference for interferometry down the line. Add references to the books used to come up with these derivations 

where $\hat a$ and $\hat b$ are orthogonal polarisation basis vectors, $z$ is the orthogonal axis which the wave progates forward with time, $t$. $E_a$ and $E_b$ are the corresponding complex amplitudes, $f$ is wave frequency, $k$ is the wavenumber, $2 \pi \lambda$. 
In short, in \cref{eq:wave_equation} the first term dictates the wave polarisation and the second term dictates the wave propagation. If a radio telescope samples the plane wave at a given instant, the polarisation term\footnote{Independent of the propagation term and associated phase.} can be described by a Jones vector, 

\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{j}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        E_a \\ 
        E_b
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    E_0 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos\psi \cos\beta - i\sin\psi \sin\beta \\
        \sin\psi \cos\beta + i\cos\psi \sin\beta
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:chap2jonesvector}
\end{equation}

Here, $\beta$ describes the shape and `handness' of the ellipse that is traced out by the wave vector, shown in \cref{fig:chpt2planewavepol}, which is what the radio telescope `sees' when it samples the wave depending on the value of $\beta$. When $\beta$ is $90^\circ$ or $0^\circ$ the wave has a linear polarisation, $\beta = \pm 45$ gives a fully circularised, with $+$ denoted has `right handed' and $-$ as `left handed'. Any other values of $\beta$ will produce elliptical traces. Varying $\psi$ in this case the polarization ellipse but doesn't changing its shape.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{chapter2/figures/plane_wave_polarization.pdf}
    \caption{The real ($\mathcal{R}$) values of the plane wave equation $a$ and $b$ components for different values of $\beta$.}
    \label{fig:chpt2planewavepol}
\end{figure}

As an instantaneous wave plane isn't observable a time averaged representation that contains observables needs to be expressed. Taking the outer product of the Jones vector with its Hermitian transpose and taking a time average yields the coherency matrix \citep{hamaker_understanding_1996} which is expressed as follows, 

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}
=
\left\langle
\mathbf{j}\mathbf{j}^{\dagger}
\right\rangle
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\langle E_a E_a^{*} \rangle &
\langle E_a E_b^{*} \rangle \\
\langle E_b E_a^{*} \rangle &
\langle E_b E_b^{*} \rangle
\end{pmatrix}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\begin{pmatrix}
I + Q & U + iV \\
U - iV & I - Q
\end{pmatrix}, 
\label{eq:chap2cohermatrix}
\end{equation}

The elements can of the coherency matrix can expressed in terms of the four Stokes parameters \citep{stokes_xxx_1852}. The Stokes parameters provide an observable relation to describe polarised light which are extensively used in radio astronomy usually in the following form. 

\begin{align}
I \equiv  & E^2 = E^2_x + E^2_y,\\ 
Q \equiv & I \cos 2\psi \ \cos 2\beta = E^2_x - E^2_y,\\
U \equiv  & I \sin 2\psi \ \cos 2\beta = 2 E_x E_y \cos 2 \phi,\\
V \equiv & I \sin 2\beta = 2 E_x E_y \sin \phi.
\end{align}

Radio telescopes in reality only measure $x$ and $y$ in the electric field plane $(E_x,E_y)$. $\psi$ is the rotation of the wave in respect to the plane of the ellipse \citep[p. 262,][]{pulsar_handbook}. 

\subsection{Observational Hardware}

In the previous section the framework for measuring an electromagnetic radio wave with a radio telescope was introduced. Here a relatively simple signal path (shown in \cref{fig:chap2RadioBlockDiagrm}) for a radio telescope is introduced, before later introducing interferometry and scaling this to large arrays.
The first part of the signal path is often referred to as the `antenna', this could be anything from a simple dipole, to a large reflector dish with a complex feed system. Incoming radio waves induce an oscillating electric current in the antenna or feed. The induced voltage is measured by the receiver and forms the primary observable in radio astronomy. However, there are numerous steps in-between. \\

Next the signal is passed to the frontend. The polarizer depends on the setup of the telescope, in some cases there will just be an $x$ and $y$ antenna pair, for a feed there is physically only the one feed, but a polariser like an orthomode transducer (OMT) is used. An OMT acts as a waveguide component that can separate the signal into $E_x$ and $E_y$. Following this both polarisations have the same receiver chains, denoted in \cref{fig:chap2RadioBlockDiagrm} by the dashed arrows. The signal is then amplified using a low noise amplifier (LNA) and put through a bandpass filter which isolates the desired part of the radio band, isolating out-of-band interference. Multiple filters can be used to filter bright sources such as the FM radio band or other sources of radio frequency interference (RFI). \\

A local oscillator generates a stable then mixes the signal with the original input changing the frequency of the signal, this is done to make the signal easier to process. For example, if processing a 1400~MHz sample the setup would need analog to digital converter (ADC)'s to match the frequency which is expensive from a processing point of view, so the signal is usually mixed to lower frequencies. The Intermediate Frequency Amplifier (IF Amp) is then used to further amplify the signal, providing additional gain to the downconverted signal to match the dynamic range inputted into the ADC. It's easier to do additional amplification here as the noise mitigation isn't as necessary \citep{warnick_generalized_2024}. The signal is then converted from an analog signal to digital time series. \\ 
It's important to note that the system just described is not a generalised signal path for a radio telescope, this is a setup that would be similar to those found in a single dish system. There are many bespoke systems specific to different observing requirements. For example with the rise in RFI across the radio spectrum there is now the implementation of real-time RFI excision as part of the signal path.

\subsection{Signal Processing}
\label{sec:chap2signalprocessing}

The next step in the signal path is to carry out signal processing. This is the final step before turning the data into a science useable product. This requires the conversion of the voltage time series from an analog signal to a digital one. The digital signal is then `channelized' into frequency bins for each time sample. This is carried out by conducting a discrete Fourier-transform (DFT). This is commonly done in radio astronomy by using a Polyphase Filterbanking (PFB). PFB's has become the technical standard in radio astronomy due to its spectral performance and computational efficiency. For a comprehensive review of signal processing and polyphase filterbanking in the context of radio astronomy please see \citet[chap. 4,][]{WilsonToolsofRadioastron} and \cite{price_spectrometers_2018}

\subsubsection{Digitization}

Digitization, or digital sampling is the process in converting an analog signal into a digital signal. This is done with an ADC, which samples the incoming voltage at regular intervals. The two main factors of an ADC that are important to a radio astronomer is the sampling rate and the bit rate. \\ 
To conserve all the information from the voltages the Nyquist crieterion must be met. This states that sample rate of the ADC must meet twice the bandwidth, failure to meet this will result in aliasing of the signal. \\ 
In addition to the sample rate the ADC also needs to quantize the amplitude of the incoming signal. Which each amplitude measured by a number of bits, such that the measured amplitude levels are $N_{\text{levels}} = 2^{n_\text{bits}}$. An important point to consider is that the difference between the true analogue voltage and the nearest digital level introduces quantisation noise. Increasing the number of bits reduces this noise and increases the dynamic range of the digitised signal, with a theoretical limit of $\text{DR} \simeq 6.02 \ n_{\text{nbits}}$. In the context of a radio telescope, the received signals are typically noise dominated and approximately Gaussian distributed. Consequently, relatively low bit depths can be used without a substantial loss in sensitivity. For example, 2-bit quantisation retains approximately 88\% of the sensitivity of an ideal unquantised system, while 4-bit quantisation retains approximately 99\%. The digitised voltage stream from the ADC forms the input to the rest of the digital signal-processing pipelin. 


\subsubsection{Polyphase Filterbanks}

Following the digitization the discrete\footnote{Here square brackets delineate discrete time signals, and round brackets are continuous. } sampled voltage stream $x[n]$ is channelized into a set of frequency bins, the most basic form of this is the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), 

\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \sum_{N-1}^{n=0} x[n] \cdot e^{-2 \pi i n \frac{k}{N}}.
\end{equation}

Here $N$ is the transform length and $k$ denotes the frequency channel. The power spectrum is given by $|X[k]|^2$. However, because the DFT is evaluated over a given window, each frequency bin will exhibit a sinc-shaped frequency response, which leads to spectral leakage between the channels. The PFB technique combines a polyphase filter structure with a fast Fourier transform (FFT), where the filtering stage improves the frequency response of the FFT channels and reduces spectral leakage between neighbouring frequency bins. The input voltage sequence $x[n]$ is first decomposed into $P$ separate phases, known as polyphase branches which can be expressed as,

\begin{equation}
    x_p[n]= x[nP+p]
\end{equation}

Where $p$ is the given branch, and each branch contains the $P$th sample of the original sequence. These branches are filtered using, 

\begin{equation}
    y_p [n] = \sum_{M - 1}^{m = 0} h_p [m] x_p [n-m]
\end{equation}

here $h_p[m]$ represents the filter coefficients applied to the $p$th branch and $M$ is the number of filter taps. This filtering stage weights the input samples to improve the frequency response of each channel and suppress sidelobes that would otherwise contribute to spectral leakage. The filtered branches are then combined through an FFT,

\begin{equation}
    Y[k,n] = \sum_{P - 1}^{p = 0} y_p [n] \cdot e^{-2 \pi i p \frac{k}{P}}. 
\end{equation}

where $k$ denotes the output frequncy channel. The FFT separates the signal into $P$ narrow frequency channels, each corresponding to a different portion of the observing bandwidth. The resulting PFB output provides a channelised representation of the signal with improved channel isolation and reduced spectral leakage compared to a standard FFT. The power spectrum is obtained from the squared magnitude of the channelised voltages, similar to the DFT given as $|Y[k]|^2$. 

Unlike a standard FFT, which performs an FFT on a single block of $P$ samples, the PFB combines information from $M$ consecutive FFT frames before the transform is applied. This additional filtering suppresses the sidelobes of the FFT channel response, reducing spectral leakage and improving channel isolation. 

So for example if one considers a telescope that observes from $110-190$ MHz. If a PFB is applied with $P = 2^{12}$, the voltage stream will be channelised 4096 channels applied. Each channel corresponding to $\sim 0.02$ MHz, with the power of each channel equal to $|Y[k]|^2$, which produces a spectrum for a single time sample, $t_0$. The complex voltages $Y[k,n]$ therefore represent the signal power as a function of frequency channel $k$ and time sample $n$. Arranging these measurements in time order forms time-frequency array where each time sample and channel has an associated power. Data that is generated and saved in this format is known as a filterbank.

\section{Interferometry}

After discussing the setup of a photon to filterbank for a single dish telescope the idea can be scaled up to interferometric arrays. So far the resolution of the radio telescope on the sky has been neglected.In reality, diffraction from the finite aperture produces a beam pattern on the sky that limits the smallest angular scales that can be resolved.  Using the small angle approximation $(\theta \ll 1 ~ \text{rad})$ the full width half max (FWHM) of a radio telescope the characteristic angular width of the beam $(\theta)$ scales as,

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \theta \sim \alpha \frac{\lambda}{D}. 
    }
\end{equation}

Where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the electromagnetic wave and $D$ is the diameter of the telescope's dish. The field of view (FoV) of the telescope is $\theta/2$. Such that the FoV is, 

\begin{equation}
    \text{FoV} = \pi \left( \frac{\theta}{2} \right)^2 = \pi \left( \frac{\alpha D}{2 \lambda} \right). 
\end{equation}

These relations are challenges across all wavelengths, but this becomes even more evident at lower frequencies. For example the \textit{Hubble Space Telescope} has an angular resolution of 0.06 arc seconds at 600 nm, with a 2.4 m mirror. To match this resolution at 150 MHz ($\sim 2$ m) it would require a dish that is 8,400 km, which would cover over 10\% of the Earth's surface if it was a feasible engineering project. The largest steerable object on Earth is the 100m Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia, this achieves 7.4 arcminutes at 1.4 GHz (21 cm), for reference the full Moon is about 30 arcminutes. The largest single dish telescope is the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) located in Guizhou, southwestern China. FAST achieves 2.5 arcminutes at 1.4 GHz, however, it is a fixed dish, requiring the feed to move to point the telescope limiting the use of the full surface depending on where the feed points. GBT and FAST are at the limits of modern engineering, building larger single dishes would be too expensive and difficult to make structurally stable. This limitation doesn't help the astronomer who wants to view the sky at higher resolutions and lower frequencies.

The way around this is through use of interferometry. Radio interferometry works on the basis of using the interference patterns of superimposed waves to extract information. In practice this uses many elements (antennas) that are spatially separated to synthesize larger aperture. First notabley used in radio astronomy by \cite{Bolton_Nature_1948} where an antenna was placed at a cliff face and used the reflections of the radio waves of the sea to create an interference pattern to observe Cygnus. With the development of both the field and modern computing, arrays of telescopes with 1,000's of elements are commonplace with next generation facilities like the Square Kilometer Array (SKA) where this number exceed 100,000. 

\subsection{Two Element Interferometer}

Starting off with the most example, a two element interferometer which is shown in \Cref{fig:chap2interferometryexample}. The two elements are positioned at $\textbf{r}_1$ and $\textbf{r}_2$ giving a baseline between them as, $\textbf{B} = \textbf{r}_2 - \textbf{r}_1$. Any far field radio waves  propagating towards the elements are given the unit vector, $\hat p$. The difference in path length between the two elements is expressed as the dot product, 

\begin{equation}
    \delta l = \textbf{B} \cdot \hat p = \textbf{B} \sin \theta.
\end{equation}

The geometric time delay between the two elements is the difference the delay in sampling the wave at two different spatial locations and is expressed as,

\begin{equation}
    \delta t = \frac{\delta l}{c}. 
\end{equation}

As discussed in \cref{sec:chap2photon2fil} the plane wave equation was introduced and defined, see \cref{eq:wave_equation}. If you evaluate this field at the two different elements positioned,$\mathbf{r}_1$ and $\mathbf{r}_2$. The received electric fields are then, 


\begin{align}
\mathbf{E}_1(t) &= \mathbf{E}(t,\mathbf{r}_1)
= \mathbf{E}_0 \exp\!\left[2\pi i\left(ft-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_1\right)\right], \\
\mathbf{E}_2(t) &= \mathbf{E}(t,\mathbf{r}_2)
= \mathbf{E}_0 \exp\!\left[2\pi i\left(ft-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_2\right)\right].
\end{align}

However as established these two measurements of the plane wave will be offset by different path lengths, so each waves will have a different phase, $\Delta \phi = \mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1)$. Given that $|\textbf{k}| = 2 \pi / \lambda$. The phase difference can then be expressed as, 

\begin{equation}
    \Delta \phi = \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda} \delta l = 2 \pi f \delta t. 
\end{equation}

Phase differences cannot\footnote{This is not entirely true, phase can be measured, but astronomical radio signals are stochastic, noise-like processes, making only the relative phase recovered through cross-correlation physically meaningful.} The instantaneous phase of the electric field is not directly observable. Instead, the relative phase information is recovered statistically by cross-correlating the voltage streams measured at each antenna. By applying a relative delay and averaging over time, the degree of similarity between the two signals can be determined, 

\begin{equation}
    R_{12} = \langle  V_1 (t) V^*_2 (t - \tau) \rangle. 
\end{equation}

$R_{12}$ is the cross-correlation between antennas 1 and 2. $V_1$ is relative complex voltage stream of antenna 1 at $t$ and $V^*_2 (t - \tau)$ is the complex conjugate of antenna 2's voltage stream at a shifted time, $\tau$. The cross-correlation reaches a maximum when the applied delay matches the geometric delay such that $\tau = \tau_g$. This result is the measured quantity used for interferometric observations referred to as a visibility and can be generally expressed for an antenna pair, $i$ and $j$ as, 

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{V_{ij} = R_{ij} = \langle  V_i (t) V^*_j (t - \tau) \rangle}.
\end{equation}

The measured value from each antenna pair depends on both the baseline and orientation with respect to the observed source. For dealing with more than two pairs of antennas its useful to introduce a co-ordinate system, fixed to the source direction such that, 

\begin{equation}
    (u, v, w) = \frac{\textbf{B}_{ij}}{\lambda}. 
\end{equation}

Where $u$ and $v$ make up a plane that is orthogonal to the source, $\lambda$ is the wavelength of emission, with $w$ points directly towards the phase centre (line of sight), the phase centre is simply the direction on the sky for which the interferometer is defined to have zero geometric delay. This is known as the $uv$-plane and each baseline samples a point in this plane. \\ 

Now the Cittert-Zernike theorem is introduced. In its simplest form, it states that the complex visibility measured by a baseline is a Fourier component of the sky brightness $(I)$, 

\begin{equation}
    V_{ij} = V (u_{ij}, v_{ij}) = \int \int I (l,m) \exp\left[ -2 \pi i (u_{ij}l + v_{ij}m) \right] ~ dl~dm.
    \label{eq:chap2CittertZernike}
\end{equation}

Where $I$ is the sky brightness in the image domain, $l$ and $m$ are cosine directions to the point on the sky. Now its been established how to measure voltages and correlate them between pairs of antennas and transform them into the image domain. By measuring visibilities for many combinations of antennas, a 2-D sampled plane in the Fourier space is formed, the inverse transform of this plane reconstructs an image of sky brightness. This forms the basis of what is seen in \cref{fig:chap2MeerKatGCimage}.

\subsection{Aperture Synthesis}

As discussed in the previous section, each baseline can sample a part of the $uv$-plane at a given time giving a unique visibility measurement. Returning to the analogy of each element acting as a `pixel', if add unique baselines the $uv$ plane can be `filled out'. Increasing the number of pixels in a digital image, increasing the number of sampled points in the $uv$-plane improves the fidelity with which the sky brightness distribution can be reconstructed.

To better fill out the $uv$-plane the Earth's rotation is used to cover more of the plane, this is shown in \cref{fig:chap2uvplaneexample}, as the Earth rotates the baselines fill in more of the $uv$-plane. In this example a Triskelion pattern is used, with 52 antennas, which can have 1326 unique baselines. Antenna patterns are chosen depending on the target, required baseline length and how much of the $uv$-plane a specific target will sweep out. As illustrated in \cref{fig:chap2uvplaneexample} the longer the Earth is allowed to rotate the more the $uv$-plane is filled. \\ 

The coverage of the $uv$-plane determines the effective point spread function of the interferometer, more specifically the distribution of the visibilities. This is commonly referred to as a synthesized beam, it can be similarly thought of as a diffraction pattern of a single element telescope. The beam itself describes the response on interferometer to a point source. The angular resolution of the synthesised beam is approximately, 

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\rm syn} \approx \frac{\lambda}{B_{\text{max}}}.
\end{equation}

Where $B_M$ is the maximum projected baseline of the array. In reality the $uv$-plane is never fully sampled, the Cittert-Zernike relation (eq. \ref{eq:chap2CittertZernike}) assumes you know the visibility function everywhere but visibilities are only measured at a finite number of locations corresponding to actual baselines.  Since the Fourier transform of an incomplete sampling pattern is not a delta function, the response of the interferometer to a point source contains additional structure surrounding the main lobe caused by Fourier ringing. These features are known as sidelobes, due to their presence the images obtained from the Fourier space are known as dirty images, corresponding to the true sky brightness distribution convolved with the synthesized `dirty beam'. There are many methods to make this dirty beam a more accurate estimate of true sky brightness, for examples see \citet[pp. 202-220,]{WilsonToolsofRadioastron}. 

\subsection{Aperture Arrays \& Beamforming}

The work in this thesis is mostly based on the use of dynamic spectra as described in \cref{sec:chap2signalprocessing}. While aperture synthesis is mainly used for reconstructing images, the same techniques can be used to increase the sensitivity of a telescope looking at a single point. Rather than Fourier transforming the measured visibilities to form an image, the signals received by each element may be delayed and combined coherently or incoherently, this process is known as beamforming. \\ 

Coherent beamforming combines the voltages from each antenna and retains both the phase and amplitude information. Whereas, incoherent beamforming sums together only the amplitudes of the voltages. Coherent beamforming is usually the ideal scenario as sensitivity scales with the number of antennas, $n$. Preserving the relative phase information between antennas allows the array to act as a single aperture, where diameter is set by the physical extent of the array. The beam that is formed on the sky is narrower in this case as the array is acting as a single aperture, with the implementation of geometric delays allow the telescope to be `steered'.  
Incoherent beamforming combines the detected powers from each element, preserving a much larger field of view at the expense of sensitivity, which in this case scales as $\sqrt{n}$. The choice between both is ultimately depending on the science case, incoherent beamforming trades sensitivity for field of view and vica versa. For example an all sky or RFI monitoring would incoherent beamforming to monitor as much of the sky instantaneously whereas a targeted pulsar observation would use coherent beamforming to maximize sensitivity. 

Following beamforming, the resulting voltage stream as discussed in \cref{sec:chap2signalprocessing}. Dynamic spectra produced from beamforming form the primary data product used throughout this thesis and are the basis for studying pulsars, fast radio bursts, and other transient radio phenomena.

Specific intensity (or brightness) is denoted by $I_\nu$, it describes the radiation per unit area per solid angle per unit frequency. More intuitively if energy, $dE$ from within the solid angle $d\Omega$ passes through the projected area (i.e. antenna surface), where the projection is $\cos \theta ~ dA$ in a time $dt$ within a narrow frequency range $d\nu$, the differential power received can be expressed as, 

\begin{equation}
    dP = \frac{dE}{dt} = I_\nu (\cos \theta ~ d A ~ d \Omega ~ d \nu). 
\end{equation}

Solving for spectral intensity, $I_\nu$ gives, 

\begin{equation}
    I_\nu = \frac{dP}{(\cos \theta d A) ~ d\nu ~ d\Omega}.
    \label{eq:spectral_intensity}
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq:spectral_intensity} is an important result as if the directional contribution over all directions subtended by the source is summed, the total spectral flux density, $S_\nu$,

\begin{equation}
    S_\nu = \int_{\text{source}} I_\nu (\theta, \phi) \cos \theta ~ d\Omega. 
\end{equation}

If the source as an angular size $\theta \ll 1 ~\text{rad}$, then small angle approximation can be used, $\cos \theta = 1$. This gives the compact source form of flux density, 

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    S_\nu = \int_{\text{source}} I_\nu (\theta, \phi) ~ d\Omega. 
    }
    \label{eq:chap2fluxcompact}
\end{equation}

For a source where \cref{eq:chap2fluxcompact} is applicable the well known inverse-square distance dependence can be introduced as the source is observed at a greater distance the source takes up a smaller patch of the sky so its angular size shrinks and therefore its solid angle decreases, $\Omega \propto d^{-2}$. It is important to note that flux density should be measured only if spectral brightness cannot (i.e. the source is unresolved). 

\Cref{eq:chap2fluxcompact} also allows for the definition of spectral luminosity, $L_\nu$. $L_\nu$ is defined as the total power per unit bandwidth radiated by a source at a given frequency, $\nu$. If the source is assumed to be isotropic and a sphere, the flux density is that same power as spectral luminosity but radiated out as the surface of a sphere with radius, $d$. Such that, ›

\begin{equation} \boxed{ 
    L_\nu = 4 \pi d^2 S_\nu 
}
\label{eq:chap2spectralluminosity}
\end{equation}

Having related the observed flux density to the intensity (source brightness), the last formalism that needs to be introduced is for brightness temperature. Brightness temperature, $T_b$, is defined so that the specific intensity can be written in the Rayleigh-Jeans form, 

\begin{equation}
    I_\nu = \frac{2k T_b \nu^2}{c^2}
    \label{eq:chap2RayleighJeans}
\end{equation}

Solving for $T_b$, 

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{ 
    T_b (\nu) = \frac{I_\nu c^2}{2 k \nu^2}.
    }
\end{equation}

The observed flux density can be expressed as an equivalent source temperature by combining \cref{eq:chap2RayleighJeans} with the effective collecting area of a telescope, $A_{\text{eff}}$, such that, 

\begin{align}
A_{\text{eff}} &= \frac{2k_B T_{\text{source}}}{S_\nu}, \\
\therefore\quad
T_{\text{source}} &= \frac{A_{\text{eff}} S_\nu}{2k_B }.
\end{align}

The equations introduced here now have established a link between the intrinsic radiation properties of a source and the flux that is possible to measure by an observer. 

\section{Radiometer Equation}

The final core concept discussed is the radiometer equation, it's derivation in this section is a specialized extension of the derivation shown in \citet[§A1.4][]{pulsar_handbook}. The radiometer equation measures the flux received by a radio telescope. At its core it is just an application of the Central Limit Theorem\footnote{The central limit theorem states that the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows, regardless of the original population's shape.}, as it describes the uncertainty in measuring a noise temperature $(\sigma_T)$, with the uncertainty decreasing as function of the number of the samples measured, 

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_T = \frac{T_{\text{sys}}}{\sqrt{n_p t_{\text{obs}} \Delta f}}. 
\end{equation}

Here $n_p$ is the number of polarisations, $t_{\text{obs}}$ is the observation time, $\Delta f$ is the bandwidth of a single measurement and  $T_{\text{sys}}$ is the system temperature which is the sum individual noise contributions. Flux density, $S$ (in Jy) received by a radio telescope is related to the measured system temperature by the telescope gain, $G$. As $S = T/G$ where $G$ is expressed a function effective area, $G = A_{\text{eff}}/{2k}$. Thus, the radiometer equation can be re-written in terms of flux density,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_S = \frac{\sigma_T}{G} = \frac{T_{\text{sys}}}{G \sqrt{n_p t_{\text{obs}} \Delta f}}.
\end{equation}

This represents the root mean squared (RMS) uncertainty in flux density due to system noise. Setting that a source should be detectable to a given S/N value. $ S_{\min} = \text{S/N}_{\min} \cdot \sigma_S$ and substituting $\sigma_S$ gives, 

\begin{equation}
    S_{\min} = \frac{\text{S/N}_{\min} \cdot T_{\text{sys}}}{G \sqrt{n_p t_{\text{obs}} \Delta f}}
    \label{eq:chap2radiometerintro}
\end{equation}


In the case of periodic emission such as a pulsar a factor representing duty cycle is introduced,

\begin{equation}
    \delta = \frac{W}{P}.
\end{equation}


Because the pulse is only present for a fraction \( \delta \) of the time, the effective S/N is reduced. Which is expressed as, 

\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{\frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}} = \sqrt{\frac{W}{P - W}}.
\end{equation}

This factor is simply added to \cref{eq:chap2radiometerintro}, 

\begin{equation}
    S_{\min} = \beta \frac{\text{S/N}_{\min} T_{\text{sys}}}{G \sqrt{n_p t_{\text{obs}} \Delta f}} \sqrt{\frac{W}{P - W}}. 
\end{equation}

A factor of \( \beta \) has also been added as a digitization loss factor. Typically, \( \beta \approx 1.16 \) for 3-level sampling according to \cite{pulsar_handbook}, but this value is system dependent.  

It is also important to note that for single pulses, such as an FRB or otherwise, the duty cycle factor is simply swapped out for a $\tau_{\text{burst}}$, which represents in the burst duration. 


Since system temperature, gain, and bandwidth vary across frequency, the above equations can be generalized by summing over all frequency bins and adding the frequency dependency where necessary. First starting with gain, 

\begin{equation}
    G(f) = 
    \frac{A_{\text{eff}}(f)}{2k} = \frac{\eta (f) \cdot A_{phys}}{2k}.
\end{equation}

Here a frequency dependence is introduced for collecting area, as for any antenna of a given size receives a smaller angular region of the sky as frequency increases. 

\begin{equation}
\boxed{
S_{\min} (f, l, b) = \frac{1}{B_{\text{total}}} \sum_{f} \left(  \frac{\left[ T_{\text{sys}}(l,b) + T_{\text{int} }(f) \right] \cdot 2 k}{A_{\text {eff} }(f)} \cdot \beta  \quad  \cdot \frac{\text{S/N}_{\text{min}}}{\sqrt{n_p t_{\text{obs}} B_{\text{channel}}}} \sqrt{\frac{W}{P - W}} \right) \Delta f}
\end{equation}



\section{LOFAR}

A significant amount of the work is carried out in this thesis using part of the LOw Frequency ARay \citep[LOFAR;][]{van_haarlem_lofar_2013}.  LOFAR is an aperture array interferometer with antennas all across Europe shown in \cref{fig:chap2lofarmap}, when the array is operating at full capacity it can rival Hubble's subarcsecond resolution. At the time of writing, there is currently no telescope in the world that can rival LOFAR at its operating frequencies. 

LOFAR is made up of 52 stations across Europe, with two more currently under construction in Italy and Bulgaria with further construction planned for the future. Each `station' is comprised of two sets of antennas, the Low Band Antennas (LBAs) which operate from 10-90~MHz and the High Band Antennas (HBAs), which operate at 110-190~MHz.  

The center of LOFAR is based near Exloo in the Netherlands on what is known as the `superterp'. The superterp consists of 6 `core' stations with a max baseline of 240~m, in close proximity to the superterp there is further 24 stations with a max baseline of 3.5~km. There are also 16 remote Dutch stations placed up to 90~km away in the northeastern region of the Netherlands. To complete the arrat there 12 international stations based around Europe with the longest current baseline being Birr to Łazy at $\sim 1890~\textrm{km}$, this baseline with be extended to $\sim 2660~\textrm{km}$ with the addition of the Bulgarian station in Rozhen.

Each of the antennas in the field all fed into a heavily sheilded cabinet or container. Here the reciever units (RCUs) and Remote Signal Processing compute boards (RSPs) are also stored. 

The RCUs are directly connected to the coaxial cable coming from the field. The RCUs select the antenna input\footnote{At the time of writing the LOFAR 2 upgrade aims to rectify this such that both can be used simultaneously.} (either HBA or LBA). The selected signal then undergoes amplification and appropriate filters are applied to the signal, it is then digitized with a 12-bit ADC at either 200~MHz or 160~MHz. 
The digitized samples are then passed to the RSP boards, these boards before all the digital signal processing at the station. This includes "first in first out" (FIFO) buffering, which holds all the packets in arrival order in a buffer to ensure that there is no signal delay added by the RCUs, following this a polyphase-filter subbanding is used and beamlets are formed, which is discussed in further depth in the next section. The data from the RSP boards is then passed to the Central Processing Unity (CEP) which outputs the packet-stream of voltages. All of this is controlled by the Local Control Unit which is a Linux pc that monitors the station hardware and controls where the beams are `steered'. The LCU is also responsible for receiving time references from the stations GPS for ensuring phase coherence across the whole array.  

\subsection{Beamforming with a LOFAR station}

There are numerous ways to observe with LOFAR as an array and also with individual stations. As discussed the main data products of interest is dynamic spectra from the HBAs which is attained by beamforming. As mentioned above the ADC can work at a 200~MHz or 160~MHz clock rate, this corresponds to a base sampling time of 5~ns and $6.2~\upmu \text{s}$. A polyphase filterbank is applied the data above channelized into sub-bands. Due Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem ($B < f_s/2$), 200~MHz sampling gives a useable bandwidth as 100~MHz. A 1024-point FFT  then divides that sampled bandwidth into 1024 spectral bins (subbands), 512 real and 512 imaginary bins (Hermitian symmetric space). Dividing the subbands across the useable bandwidth gives 195.3~kHz to each subband. Following channelization, the station beamformer selects the desired subbands and coherently sums the antenna signals in the direction of interest. The resulting beamformed subbands are called beamlets, and in 8-bit mode LOFAR can form up to 488 beamlets. Although the polyphase filter produces 512 subbands, the station beamformer's ring architecture and output allocation mean that only 488 beamlets are available. 

One unique thing about aperture arrays is that each beamlet can be individually pointed, this means that up to 488 beams can be formed on the sky with the bandwidth of one subband or one single coherent beam can be placed on the sky using the full bandwidth as shown in \cref{fig:chap2lofarbeams}. Recalling that the S/N scaled with the number of channels by a factor of $\sqrt{n}$, the stations are primarily used with all beamlets on the same patch of sky.

\subsection{The Irish LOFAR Station}

The Irish LOFAR (I-LOFAR) station is based at the Rosse Observatory in Birr Castle, Co. Offaly about 140~km south west of Dublin. It was built by a group of students in the Summer of 2017, with first light in July of that year. Officially joining the LOFAR array as the most western station and longest baseline pair. 

LOFAR as an array operated in two different modes, an international and local mode. The international mode uses all 52 stations to form one coherent telescope. Observing time on the entire array is allocated through a peer reviewed cycle process similar to most other international facilities. However, for up to 3 days a week the stations are returned to the host institutions for what is known as `local mode'. In this mode the international stations can act independently and carry out observations on targets at the discretion of each station's consortium. Each international stations are powerful telescopes in their own right. 

The biggest shortcoming of local mode observations is the lack in resolution when imaging due to the small baselines of 10's of meters. As a result the main science products from local mode come from dynamic spectra mainly pertaining to transient searches, pulsar timing, solar system emission and the sun.  

In the case of I-LOFAR there are two backends deployed on the telescope the Real-Time Acquisition Cluster (REALTA\footnote{Réalta is Irish Gaeilge for star.}) and the Breakthrough Listen cluster. The REALTA cluster is the primary observing backend for I-LOFAR, this is the primary storage and compute nodes for most observations.  

The cluster itself is broken up into 5 servers each adorned with the name of the university that funded them. UCC1--4 are the work horses of the cluster each equipped with Nvidia Tesla V100 16GB Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), two Intel Xeon Gold 6130's Computer Processing Units (CPUs) and 256 GB of Random Access Memory (RAM). Each of the UCC's is setup in either a RAID 0 or RAID 5 configuration depending on the need for redundancy or write speeds. UCC1 is designated as the recording machine, which directly ingests CEP data streams from the telescope itself and processes them into science useable products. The other three UCC's are used for data analysis with specific radio astronomy software suites. The final machine on REALTA, NUIG1 is Just a Bunch of Discs (JBOD), it acts as a longer term archive for raw and processed data with 130~TB storage capacity. 

The Breakthrough Listen backend is just one compute node and used specifically for SETI and any ancillary science that comes from the data collected. This is equipped with a Nvidia RTX 2080Ti 11~GB and twp Intel Xeon Silver 4110's. This server can also direct ingest CEP data streams for processing as well to create SETI specfic data products (discussed further in \cref{sec:SETIchapter}).

For an in-depth review of the development of I-LOFAR's backend see \citet[chap. 3,][]{David_thesis_2023} and \cite{RealTa_paper}. 

\subsection{Upgrading to LOFAR 2.0}

In September of 2024 LOFAR paused observing with the whole array to start the rollout of a new upgrade. Dubbed LOFAR 2.0 the upgrade main goals are to increase the observing capability of each station and improve the central processing for the array as a whole.

The most significant upgrade is the replacement of all the RCUs to enable simultaneous recording of both the HBA and LBA antennas, allowing for full 30--240~MHz of observing, this also extends to keeping voltages from both antennas in the ring buffer. The increased computing capacity allows for better methods of calibration at the lower end of the band improving the sensitivity of the LBAs by a factor of 5 in some fields. The existing timing distribution is being replaced by a central clock signal distributed over a White Rabbit optical fiber network which will improve ionospheric calibration. 

The array's central processing unit COBALT, is also getting refreshed with new GPUs and CPUs providing an order of magnitude increase in the available computing resources. The new hardware and software changes allow more parallel observing modes, including simultaneous imaging and beam-formed data products from different subsets of stations. Additionally a large effort is being made to make many quality of life implementations to station control and monitoring. 

For a full discussion on the technical details and the effects on both the science cases and usability of the LOFAR upgrade see \cite{LOFAR2}. 

At the time of writing core stations have been upgraded to LOFAR 2.0 and the upgrade is being rolled out to the international stations with the Swedish station currently the first to have deployed the upgrade. The current expected completion of the LOFAR 2.0 upgrade is Q4 2027. While the upgrade has been carried out the international stations have been operating in local mode 24/7, which has allowed for prolonged observation campaigns on various science projects. For example this work took advantage of this fact and used both the Swedish and Irish station for a prolonged survey which is discussed in \cref{chap:LOFTS}.

\subsection{NenuFAR}

The New Extension in Nançay Upgrading LOFAR (NenuFAR) is located at the Naçay radio observatory in France. Located next to the French LOFAR station. NenuFAR operates at the same frequencies as the LBAs but uses a different design for its antennas and amplifiers (see. \cref{fig:chap2nenufar}). The  antennas were intially manufactured for the Long Wavelength Array (LWA) in Owen's Valley, with the novel amplifiers designed in Naçay.  The station shares much of the same electronics and systems as a standard LOFAR stations, so the description outlined above can also be applied here. The combination of both the antennas and the amplifiers translated to a flatter response curve than that of the LBAs. At present there are X out of a planned 96 array core constructed. Similarly, to LOFAR's core there are also remote stations called mini arrays (MAs) surrounding NenuFAR. Each MA consists of 19 antennas and is placed up to 3~km from the NenuFAR core. The telescope can also be used in tandem with the LOFAR array to add NenuFAR's sensitivity along with the other stations.
