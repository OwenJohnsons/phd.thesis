\chapter{A LOw Frequency pulsar, FRB, and Technosignature Survey} \label{chap:LOFTS}

This chapter describes the planning and execution of the LOw Frequency Pulsar, Fast Radio Burst, and Technosignature Survey (LOFTS). The work presented forms part of a broader collaborative programme comprising multiple ongoing studies, in which the author has contributed as either the lead or a co-author. The chapter is based on a manuscript currently in preparation but has been substantially revised and expanded to avoid repetition with the material presented in the previous chapter and to provide additional context where appropriate.

The final section of this chapter presents work carried out over the past several years on the first technosignature search conducted with the NenuFAR radio telescope and current progress of the project.


\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

\Cref{sec:SETIchapter} outlined the first dual-site technosignature survey using LOFAR. This study demonstrated the advantages of using geographically distant telescopes to reject terrestrial interference while also setting the most stringent constraints on narrowband technosignatures in the low frequency regime $(<200~\text{MHz})$. The next step for continuing this work was to observe for longer, search for more types of transients both natural and artifical along with implementing further improvements that have been established in the literature since the first survey's observations were carried out. 

\subsection{Motivation}

The broader motivations for doing technosignature surveys remain the same as previously outlined. Historically, technosignature surveys have utilized large single-dish telescopes operating at frequencies above 1 GHz \citep{Siemion_KEPLER_ApJ, Price2020}. Technosignature searches often target narrowband radio emissions, but there is no \textit{a priori} reason to prefer any specific part of the radio spectrum, necessitating surveys across a broad range from 30 MHz to 100 GHz \citep[\cref{fig:chap1SETIfreq},][]{Ng2022}. This also extends to the type of technosigantures that are searched for, as will be later discussed, LOFTS extends the technosignature search to two other type of technosignatures that have previously been neglected at this frequency. 

This survey was designed with also with ancillary science in mind. Technosignature survey's can in tandem with technosignatures focus on other transient phenomena, such as pulsars, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), long period transients (LPTs) and flare stars. By targeting both technosignatures and other transients, the LOFTS survey aims to maximize its scientific impact, contributing valuable data to multiple areas of astrophysics. 

This survey was also motivated by the circumstances of the upgrade to LOFAR2.0\footnote{\href{https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://www.lofar.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/LOFAR2_0_White_Paper_v2023.1.pdf&ved=2ahUKEwjHnrOH3ciHAxU-EkQIHfa6DJUQFnoECBEQAQ&usg=AOvVaw0ze94uccrly_yfSru4PsCZ}{LOFAR2.0 White Paper - v2023.1}}. As mentioned in \cref{sec:upgradetoLOFAR2}, LOFAR typically operates in international mode, utilizing all stations across Europe. As a result, the stations only operate in standalone mode on average two days a week, which allows for smaller-scale projects coordinated by science users. However, the commissioning of LOFAR 2.0 began in September 2024 at the core stations in Dwingeloo. Since then, international stations have been operating in standalone mode 24/7, providing an abundance of observing time for projects such as LOFTS.

\subsection{Technosignatures}
 \cite{johnson_simultaneous_2023} employed international stations, SE607 and IE613 to conduct the most comprehensive search for technosignatures at sub 650~MHz. Using LOFAR's wide field of view \citep{LOFAR_instrument}, over 1.6 million stars were observed over the course of 10 hours of observations. This study also showcased how dual-site observations can be used to eliminate all narrowband false positives from the observations. This resulted in the tightest constraints ever produced in the emergence of intelligent civilizations in this frequency regime.

The next step for a technosignature survey at low frequencies is to significantly expand the volume of stars observed by covering more of the sky. This is achieved by increasing both the time of each observation and the quantity of observations using both stations. In addition to narrowband signals, two more types of technosignatures will also be surveyed, such as periodic spectral signals \citep{edmondson_utilization_2003, suresh_48_2023} and artificially dispersed transient signals \citep{2022VishalPulsed}. 

The former proposes that rather than transmitting a continuous narrowband signal, an engineered transmitter could intentionally modulate in emission and time, producing a periodic pattern that is easily characterized as artificial (i.e. radar communications). The signal itself consists of a narrowband carried that intensities turns on and off at some given interval. The period itself depends on the transmitter design but taking PSS signals on Earth as example this would be somewhere within the range of milliseconds to seconds. 

Whereas the latter uses FRBs as a starting point. In this context its thought that a signal might be intentionally engineered to mimic that of dispersed signal, like FRBs. The anthropomorphic thing about these signals from nature is that they would exhibit either an inverted DM sweep (transposed frequency), inverted sweep in time (transposed time) or both, shown in \cref{fig:artificalFRBs}. Other civilisations may intentionally mimic a signal due to its unnatural characteristics, there is also the argument to be made that a broad band signal has less chance of being missed in comparison to its narrowband counterparts. There has also been some discussion of what technology such a signal could be a byproduct of, this includes light sail propulsion or some high-powered radio beam but as with all technosignatures this remains speculative \citep{lingam_fast_2017}.

\subsection{Transients}

International LOFAR stations are able to detect novel transients in their own right \citep[i.e.][]{MCKENNA}, making the these stations equally valuable to transients science as technosignatures. As will later be outlined in this chapter an array of data products are generated to match both the frequency and temporal resolutions required to search for a plethora of transients. This includes Pulsars, RRATs, LPTs, FRBs and radio flare stars. 

Searching for these objects is the primary driver in the increased observation times for each pointing in this survey from 15 to 80 minutes.

\section{Observation Strategy}\label{sec:LOFTSobs}

A total of 12--24 hours a week have been allocated to LOFTS since October 2024. This takes place on Wednesdays from 18:00 for 24 hours. On weeks when solar observations take precedence, LOFTS observations are run for 12 hours during the nights to minimize the amount of RFI. Due to the nature of the narrowband drifting signals that are expected from technosignatures, the use of a drift scan observation approach is untenable. This is due to the longer time samples and that a centred star would remain within the FWHM for only 20~mins at 150~MHz if centred on bore sight. There is approximately a difference of 80~mins in Local Sidereal Time (LST) between the Irish and Swedish stations. To maximise the sensitivity each pointing starts 40 minutes before zenith the midpoint in latitude and longitude and tracks the sky for 80 minutes. The following observation then re-starts again at that point, and so on. This means that at some point in the observation the patch of sky being observed is at zenith for both the stations either at the start (Ireland) or end (Sweden) of the observation. When then declination has been sufficiently sampled observations are then moved by $\pm2.59^\circ$ (FWHM at 150~MHz) to cover new regions of the sky (see his observation time has been chosen to increase the sensitivity to each of the transients of interest and align with the difference in local sidereal time (LST) between the two stations, which is also 80 minutes. The HBA beam sensitivity of a LOFAR station can be roughly approximated with respect to zenith angle with the following formula,

\begin{equation}
    S(\theta) = S_0 \cdot \cos^2 \theta_{ZA}
    \label{eq:cos2za}
\end{equation}

Where $S_0$ is zenith sensitivity which optimizes sensitivity because the effective area increases as the angular separation from the zenith decreases. In reality modelling the response of a LOFAR station is quite complex and difficult to accurately determine for a comprehensive overview of how HBA sensitivity is characterised, please see \cite{Vincetti2026}. Every 8 hours there is also a 12--15 minute observation of B1508+55 or B0329+54 dependent on LST, this is done for pipeline verification purposes. If any of the observations are found to have emission of interest manual follow-up will be carried out with both stations.

\subsection{Data Collection and Processing}

Observations are executed using iLiSA, which records received UDP packets of beam-formed data and writes them to disk both in Ireland and Sweden similarly to \cref{sec:SETIchapter}. The recorded data are coarse-channelized complex voltages recorded using LOFAR's $200$-MHz clock~\citep{LOFAR_instrument}, with native frequency and time resolutions of $195.3125$~kHz and $5.12\;\upmu$s respectively. Due to the increased observing cadence of LOFTS a fully automated processing pipeline\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OwenJohnsons/LOFTS-Scripts}} was developed to generate the science data products and remove voltages. HBA voltage data is about $3.04$~GB/sec (uncompressed) which rapidly fills up disks and bottlenecks the backend. A high-level overview of the pipeline can be seen \cref{fig:LOFTSpipeline}, the voltages in the pipeline were handled in the same way as outlined in \cref{sec:SETI_obs_descrip}, for this study ancillary science data products were refined and produced to the specifications as shown in \cref{fig:LOFTSpipeline}. Once the data is generated it is sorted by date and placed into `short' term storage for data analysis on the BL backend. Filterbanks aren't deleted off disk, but due to the age of the system and lifetime of hard disk drives, as much of the data is transferred to Linear Tape-Open (LTO) tapes to retain redundancy. LTO tapes are rated to have an expected lifetime of 25+ years, making them viable long term storage solution.  I-LOFAR's long term archive is called `archie' which is based in Technical University of the Shannon in Athlone which has a direct fibre link to I-LOFAR in Birr.

\subsection{RFI Excision} \label{sec:RFI}

Radio frequency interference (RFI) plagues all modern radio astronomy data as outlined throughout this thesis. In the case of searching for new transients its vital to remove as much persistent and periodic RFI as possible, if the noise floor is too high many natural signals from novel targets will be missed. In addition to using tools like \textsc{rfimask} \citep{ransom_new_2001} some preliminary work was carried out to explore other ways clean the data. One method that was tested was to take an averaged spectrum every 10\% ($\sim 8~\rm mins$) of each observation using the narrowband data product, $f_\text{res} \sim 3 ~\rm Hz$.  These spectra were then compared to an ideal (no RFI) HBA band-pass. Then several statistical properties of every frequency channel were calculated both relative to nearby channels and throughout the entire observation. An Isolation Forest was then used to identify which channel statistics differed significantly from the majority, marking them as anomalous. Initial test where any temporal or frequency anomalies that were above $6\sigma$ for more than 60\% of each 10\% chunk are marked as RFI. Any channels that were present had RFI present for more than 80\% of the total LOFTS observations were permanently masked during observations. An example of a mask generated from a LOFTS observation is shown in \cref{fig:RFI-mask}. Other more complex methods such as convolution neural networks (CNNs) and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) were tested but produced diminishing returns for their added complexity, labelling and resulting mask. These masks worked well on the test observations removing most notable RFI when examined by eye. The next extension of this work would to carry out S/N recovery of pulsar observations using different various RFI methods and combinations. There is also room to experiment with other classical machine learning methods like Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCANs) to compare their computational and masking efficiency against the provisional results.

\subsection{De-dispersion} \label{sec:DDisperse}

For the non-artificial fast transients (FRBs, LPTs, RRATs and Pulsars) the data must be de-dispersed to account for pulse broadening that occurs due to propagation through the ISM known as DM as discussed in \cref{sec:DispersionMeasure}. Without these corrections, pulses would be smeared, resulting in a loss of signal-to-noise and potential non-detection of an otherwise detectable pulse. DM accuracy changes as a function of pulse-width so when using the HBA the relative S/N loss decreases significantly with pulse width, becoming negligible 45.32~seconds as shown in \cref{fig:LOFTS_HBA_graphs}.  Pulse widths of this order are expected from LPTs. The values used for de-dispersing for this survey DM values from 0-600 \dm are processed. The DM ceiling is chosen due to the dominance of scattering at the lower end of the band, which is the majority contribution $(844.21~\rm ms)$ at 600 \dm. For an in-depth de-dispersion plan and means of calculations, please see appendix \ref{appen:low-frequency-dd}.

\section{Technosignature Detection} \label{sec:LOFTSanalysis}

% This survey searches for three forms of technosignature, narrowband, periodic spectrally modulated signals and dispersed signals. 
Continuous narrowband signals have been the primary target of existing searches \citep{Siemion_KEPLER_ApJ, Gajjar_2021_BLGC1} due to their absence in nature, and the assumption of viability of interstellar messaging by this means \citep{CocconiMorrison1959, Sagan1966}. In addition to narrowband signals, this survey searches for artificially dispersed broadband bursts \citep{2022VishalPulsed} and periodically pulsed beacons \citep{suresh_48_2023}.

\subsection{Narrowband Technosignatures}

Since the work was carried out in \cref{sec:SETIchapter} the development of \textsc{TurboSETI} has been succeeded by the Breakthrough Listen Interesting Signal Search (\textsc{BLISS})\footnote{\url{https://github.com/n-west/bliss}}. BLISS improves signal recovery and parameter estimation by using better noise handling, more accurate SNR estimates, and clustering logic that helps merge nearby hits while reducing false positives.

As before the Doppler-drift search was carried out on barycentred high time resolution data products at both sites. This time the drift-rate search is carried out at $\pm 7.98~\rm Hz~s^{-1}$ based on the recommended values presented in \cite{li_developing_2023}. These candidate lists are then compared against each-other for dual-site coincidence rejection to see if there are any narrowband signals within $\pm 4\rm ~Hz$ in frequency and $\pm 0.2~\rm Hz~s^{-1}$.

In addition to the newly developed pipelines for searching for technosignatures there has been some work published by \cite{gajjar_exoipm_2026}. This works presents the case that interstellar and circumstellar plasma causes scattering in narrowband technosignatures making them broaden and thus harder to detect. A framework is also proposed to model to estimate extent that occultation and scintillation effects. This model derives expressions for stellar wind speed, turbulence and radial distance of a transmitter from the star along with observing frequency and line of sight geometry. This model was tested in a million star Monte-Carlo survey to which the results found that for transmitters in Sun-like stars produced nonnegligible broadening at low frequencies $(\sim100~\text{MHz})$ with this effect becoming more severe for M-dwarfs with 60\% of the entire model population 

To address this issue at low frequencies the one the potential solutions is to conduct the narrowband searches with a Lorentzian function, 

\begin{equation}
    L(x) = \frac{A \gamma}{(x-x_0)^2 +\gamma^2}.
\end{equation}

Where $A$ is the amplitude, $x_0$ is the central frequency, and $\gamma$ is the half-width at half-maximum (HWHM), with $\mathrm{FWHM}=2\gamma$. Presently there is ongoing work that injected broaden functions into LOFTS data and benchmarks the recovery performance of the standard \textsc{BLISS} pipeline and an extension of \textsc{BLISS} that uses a Lorentzian function to retrieve the signal. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{chapter6/figures/fig3_standard_detectability.png}
    \caption[BLISS detection sensitivity]{The 50\% detection threshold of the standard \textsc{BLISS} search as a function of injected signal width and Doppler drift rate. Wider signals require progressively larger injected SNRs to achieve the same detection probability, demonstrating the sensitivity loss caused by spectral broadening. Credit Naoise Lowry}
    \label{fig:lorennbcontour}

This loss in sensitivity motivates the use of a width-aware matched filter \cref{fig:lorennbcontour} using a Lorentzian template. Rather than detecting only the brightest channel, the matched filter combines power across the full broadened profile of the signal, recovering a significant fraction of the sensitivity lost due to spectral broadening. 

\subsection{Artificially Dispersed Technosignatures}

As mentioned another technosignature that can be searched for are those that are artificially dispersed. This is done using \textsc{SPANDAK}\footnote{Spandak is hindu for pulsator.} \citep{2022VishalPulsed} which is a GPU-accelerated single pulse search pipeline purpose built to find broadband pulsed technosignatures but also functions as an FRB or single pulse dectector as it is a modified version of Heimdall \citep{barsdell_accelerating_2012}. Heimdall is a software the de-disperes radio time series across an array of trial DMs. It then fits a boxcar function to the time series  looking for peaks in the de-dispersed data.  If the peak meets a certain S/N threshold which is set by the user (in this case 7.3) a list of candidates that meet this threshold are produced. These candidates have an associated DM, SNR, width and pulse time. 

This pulse search engine forms the core of SPANDAK. However, SPANDAK adds transformations to the inputted data by transposing the time, frequency and both axes to search for the three artificial dispersed signals outlined in the previous section. SPANDAK also does several consistency checks on each candidate by checking both the DM and SNR, candidates are grouped by proximity in time, dispersion measure, and signal-to-noise ratio with detections that fall within the same cluster are consolidated and reported as a single event. The resulting candidates set is then passed to a convolution neural network classifier. which ranks each of the events by how closely their de-disperesed time-frequency morphology matches a true broadband pulse. An example of SPANDAK being used on the Irish BL backend to detect a single pulse from B2217+47 is shown in \cref{fig:SPANDAK_example}.

\subsection{Periodic Technosignatures}

The final technosignature searched for as part of the LOFTS survey is the periodically modulated signals. These signals use the Breakthrough Listen Investigation for Periodic Spectral Signals \citep[\textsc{blipps};][]{suresh_48_2023}, which is a CPU Fast Folding Algorithm (FFA). Instead of isolated pulses this \textsc{blipps} operates similarly to the FFA methods used in \cref{chap:redback}.  \textsc{blipss} operates on channelized dynamic spectra and searches each frequency channel for periodic pulse trains over a trial period range, then groups harmonically related candidates and applies on-source/off-source consistency tests to suppress radio-frequency interference. Candidates that survive these cuts are folded and visually inspected, allowing the pipeline to identify periodic technosignatures even when their emission is swept in frequency or buried in intermittent interference.

As the dual-site observational setup doesn't use the on and off source tests a fork\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OwenJohnsons/blipss.lofar}} of \textsc{blipps} was made and adapted to be easily integrated into the processing pipeline. Unlike traditional pipelines that rely on precise geometric alignment or strict feature engineering, this approach utilizes a Siamese U-Net architecture to learn a generalized metric of signal similarity. A Siamese network is a neural network that is designed to determine whether two inputs represent the same underlying object or event, in this case it's a narrowband signal. It does this by passing each input through two identical networks that produce feature representations in a shared latent space, where similar inputs are placed close together and dissimilar inputs are placed farther apart. In the context of this work, the network compares dynamic spectra from the Irish and Swedish LOFAR stations to determine whether candidate signals are consistent with a common astrophysical origin or are likely to be local RFI. If they are common they will be closer in the latent space and further away if uncommon. 

A common analogy often used is that Siamese nets are good at telling if two different fingerprints are from the same hand but not who that hand belongs to, so it can identify if the signal originates from the same place, but not its origin. 

The model ingests filterbank slices with the same starting time sample from both stations. A U-Net backbone (see. \cref{fig:Unet_arch}) is then used. A U-net encoder consists of two parts an encoder and decoder. The encoder progressively downsamples the given input while learning about various features (i.e. narrow lines from a narrowband signal) with each progressive layer the complexity of the features learned increases. The decoder then upsamples these learned features back to their original resolution. This is done with the aid of skip connections which are feature maps that describe the input at that particular downsampled resolution. 
The benefit of using the U-net architecture is that it preserves the high resolution time and frequency structure of the signal during feature extraction.

These representative features are then compressed into a numerical representation and stored in vectors of a fixed-length (in this case 512), this process is known as embedding. The embedded vectors are put into a shared 512-dimensional latent space. A contrastive loss function is used to train the network by evaluating how similar or dissimilar pairs of input signals should be. During training, pairs of observations known to originate from the same astrophysical signal are encouraged to produce similar embeddings, while unrelated signals, such as local RFI, are encouraged to produce distinct embeddings. This process organises the latent space such that genuine coincident signals lie close together in the latent space, whereas unrelated signals are separated. The margin (distance) threshold is expressed as $\tau$ and matches are filtered as, 

\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
d < \tau, & \text{if the pair is a match},\\[6pt]
d \geq \tau, & \text{if the pair is not a match (RFI)}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This method learns to recognise matching signals even when they differ slightly in morphology because of scintillation or changes in local noise between the station, oppose to the alternative sample by sample cross correlation. Preliminary testing with synthetic narrowband and pulsed signals injected into real LOFAR observations shows that the network can reliably distinguish genuine coincident signals from unrelated RFI for S/N ratios between 1.5 and 10 which makes it a very promising tool for the future as shown in \cref{fig:unetHistogram.pdf}

\subsection{Dispersed Signal Detection}

This survey also searched for pulsars through acceleration searching and single burst searches see \cref{chap:redback}. The data is de-dispersion and RFI-excised as described in §\ref{sec:RFI} and §\ref{sec:DDisperse}. Then data is then analysed for single burst emission using \textsc{transientX} \citep{men_transientx_2024} and \textsc{singburst.py} \citep{ransom_presto_2011}. Here, the minimum threshold considered is those that are above the noise floor, which is 7.4 for an 80-minute observation with the high time resolution filterbank. Candidate lists between both stations in the case of simultaneous observations are then compared for matched candidates. Blind periodicity searches are also carried out as well going from twice the time resolution of the data (1.31~ms) to meet the Nyquist criterion. Periods are also searched up to 20~minutes. Its also worth noting that the computation increases with the size of period with smaller periods being significantly more computationally intensive and reducing in required resources as the period increases such that, 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dN_{\text{trial}}}{dP} \propto \frac{T_{\text{obs}}}{P^2}
\end{equation}

where $dN_{\rm trial}$ is the number of independent trial periods within the interval $dP$, $P$ is the trial period, and $T_{\rm obs}$ is the total observation duration. For more in depth discussion about the performance of FFT and FFA pulsar searching algorithms please see \cite{cameron_investigation_2017}.

In addition to this preliminary work has been ongoing to test the limits of what pulsars can be found in the HBA data. \textsc{simpulse}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OwenJohnsons/simpulse}} has been developed based off of \cite{qiu_systematic_2023} to inject both single and periodic pulses into filterbank data to test both search algorithms and the limits of how feint, fast or broad a radio transient can be to be detectable with LOFAR. This methodology also has other benefits that could be used in other studies, such as extending the work in \cref{chap:crabpulsar} by further modelling and improving both scattering and DM retrievals. Another posible use is to inject mulitple pulsars into a single beam and design a pipeline to isolate the signal of each pulsars, a pipeline like this could lend itself to pulsar searches in globular clusters where there is already multiple bright known pulsars in the FoV disrupting further pulsar searches. 

\subsection{Stellar Flare Detection}
Another transient of interest is stellar flare stars which have been known to produce emission in LOFAR's HBA band \citep[i.e.][]{CR_Draconis_LOFAR}. As discussed in \cref{sec:RadioFlareStars} radio flare stars interesting objects in their own right with many open questions. As part of the observation campaign two systems were monitored for potential flares along with each of the LOFTS pointings. 

To search for these systems a separate pipeline was built to be integrated with LOFTS initial pipeline called \textsc{stellarwaves}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/OwenJohnsons/stellar-waves}}, this pipeline gives users the means to clean, downsample and plot data across various polarisations and timescales through a simple python command line interface. Initial efforts to introduce match filtering were implemented but soon completely omitted as flare stars exhibit varied morphology and due to both the broadband nature of the emission and the timescale of the bursts manually observing plots is time efficient. 

To first obtain a baseline of how well a single international station could detect a flare star over 12 hours of data was taken of CR Draconis. CR Draconis is a known radio loud flare star binary, that's exhibits strong magnetic activity and low frequency ECM emission \citep{CR_Draconis_LOFAR}. The system has previously detected at ~168-176~MHz with the LOFAR core and has been noted as having a peak flux of up to 200~MJy with an estimated flare rate of 2.3 with 90\% of those flares detected in radio to $3\sigma$. However, no flaring emission was detected to reasonable certainty. 

Similarly, 6 hours of observations of TVLM 513-46, which is an ultracool dwarf near a stellar brown dwarf 10.7~pc away \citep{hallinan_rotational_2006}. Also noted for the ECM emission and strong magnetic fields, it is often considered a benchmark for studying stellar magnetospheres. At the time of writing there has been no reported low frequency emission of TVLM 513-46 \citep{jaeger_325_2011}. As with CR Draconis there was no notable emission detected above the noise floor in each of the observation runs with both stations.
