\chapter{A LOw Frequency pulsar, FRB, and Technosignature Survey} \label{chap:LOFTS}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

The search for technosignatures, evidence of extraterrestrial life, addresses one of the most profound questions in science: Are we alone in the Universe? Concurrently, the recent flourishing of the field of radio transients has significantly advanced our understanding of various areas of physics. Notably, pulsars and fast radio bursts (FRBs) stand out due to their unique potential to uncover new kinds of physics. These astrophysical phenomena provide valuable insights into gravitational waves, cosmology, and plasma physics, among other fields. All of the above provides the basis for conducting a LOw Frequency pulsar and Technosignature Survey (LOFTS), which aims to search for a range of technosignatures and other transients at 110--190 MHz.

\subsection{Motivation}
Historically, technosignature surveys have utilized large single-dish telescopes operating at frequencies above 1 GHz \citep{Siemion_KEPLER_ApJ, Price2020}. Technosignature searches often target narrowband radio emissions, but there is no \textit{a priori} reason to prefer fany specific part of the radio spectrum, necessitating surveys across a broad range from 30 MHz to 100 GHz \citep{Ng2022}.

Low-frequency radio SETI faces challenges due to higher sky temperatures that limit observational sensitivity. But the LOw Frequency ARray (LOFAR,~\citealt{LOFAR_instrument}) offers a strong scientific case for SETI surveys due to its sensitivity and its large field-of-view which enables observations of thousands of stars per epoch~\citep{Loeb2007}. One major issue in radio SETI is managing radio frequency interference (RFI), which traditional single-dish radio telescopes struggle with unless equipped with multi-beam receivers. These telescopes use an `ON' and `OFF' observing technique to distinguish between interference and genuine astrophysical signals.

Using two far-separated LOFAR stations provides significant advantages. This approach benefits from differing local RFI environments, allowing for more effective rejection of false-positive signals without needing the `ON' and `OFF' regime. As a result, the entire observation time can be dedicated to directly observing targets. This makes multi-station SETI surveys particularly valuable in the ever worsening RFI-laden environment \citep{johnson_simultaneous_2023}.

Moreover, the technosignature survey's can in tandem with technosignatures focus on other transient phenomena, such as pulsars, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), long period transients (LPTs) and flare stars. By targeting both technosignatures and other transients, the LOFTS survey aims to maximize its scientific impact, contributing valuable data to multiple areas of astrophysics. 

The present time and impending upgrade to LOFAR2.0\footnote{\href{https://www.google.com/url?sa=t&source=web&rct=j&opi=89978449&url=https://www.lofar.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/LOFAR2_0_White_Paper_v2023.1.pdf&ved=2ahUKEwjHnrOH3ciHAxU-EkQIHfa6DJUQFnoECBEQAQ&usg=AOvVaw0ze94uccrly_yfSru4PsCZ}{LOFAR2.0 White Paper - v2023.1}} is a strong motivator for this survey. LOFAR typically operates in international mode, utilizing all stations across Europe. As a result, the stations only operate in standalone mode on average two days a week, which allows for smaller-scale projects coordinated by science users. However, the commissioning of LOFAR 2.0 is expected to begin in Q4 2024 at the main core stations in Dwingeloo. During this period, international stations will operate in standalone mode 24/7, providing an abundance of observation time for projects such as LOFTS.

\section{Observations}\label{sec:obs}

\subsection{Observation Strategy}

A total of 12--24 hours a week have been allocated to LOFTS since October 2024. This takes place on Wednesdays from 18:00 for 24 hours. On weeks when solar observations take precedence, LOFTS observations are run for 12 hours during the nights to minimize the amount of RFI. Due to the nature of the narrowband drifting signals that are expected from technosignatures, the use of a drift scan observation approach is untenable. This is due to the long time samples . Thus, each pointing starts 40 minutes before zenith in Ireland and tracks the sky for 80 minutes, then re-start the following pointing at zenith, and so on. This observation time has been chosen to increase the sensitivity to each of the transients of interest and align with the difference in local sidereal time (LST) between the two stations, which is also 80 minutes. Every 8 hours there is also a 12--15 minute observation of B1508+55 or B0329+54 dependent on LST, this is done for verification purposes. The beam sensitivity of a LOFAR station can be approximated with respect to zenith angle with the following formula,

\begin{equation}
    S(\theta) = S_0 \cdot \cos^2 \theta \cdot e^{-\tau(\theta, f)}
\end{equation}

Where $S_0$ is zenith sensitivity  when $\theta = 0$. $\tau(\theta)$ represents atmospheric effects, and as LOFAR operates at lower frequencies, this is dominated by ionospheric effects.\\ 
With near-zenith pointings, $S_0$ is closely emulated, which optimizes sensitivity because the effective area increases as the angular separation from the zenith decreases. For a comprehensive overview of how the HBA sensitivity is characterised, please see Vincetti et al. (in prep). Any emission signatures that match any of the previously mentioned targets will be flagged for further follow-up observation.

\subsection{RFI Excision} \label{sec:RFI}

Radio frequency interference (RFI) plagues all modern radio astronomy data. To best clean the data for use in this survey, first averaged spectra were taken every 10\% ($\sim 8~\rm mins$) of the observation and compared to an ideal HBA band-pass. Local and global statistical features are identified and using iterative isolation forest method to identify anomalous frequency channels \nref. These channels are then masked permanently. In preparation for acceleration searches, the data is further cleaned using \textsc{rfifind} \citep{ransom_new_2001} where any temporal or frequency anomalies that are above $6\sigma$ for more than 60\% of each 10-second chunk are marked as RFI. \subsection{De-dispersion} \label{sec:DDisperse}

For the non-artificial fast transients (FRBs, LPTs, RRATs and Pulsars) the data must be de-dispersed to account for pulse broadening that occurs due to propagation through the ISM known as Dispersion Measure (DM). Without these corrections, pulses would be smeared, resulting in a loss of signal-to-noise and potential non-detection of a detectable pulse, except longer transients of X pulse width. The values used for de-dispersing for this survey DM values from 0-600 \dm are processed. The DM ceiling is chosen due to the dominance of scattering at the lower end of the band, which is the majority contribution $(844.21~\rm ms)$ at 600 \dm. For an in-depth de-dispersion plan and means of calculations, please see appendix \ref{appen:low-frequency-dd}.

\subsubsection{Deep Learning-Based Coincidence Matching} \label{sec:ml_rfi}

In addition to the standard Doppler-drift searches described in \S 3.1.1, we employ a deep learning framework to automate dual-site coincidence matching using Siamese Networks \citep[e.g.][]{Bromley1993, Koch2015}. Unlike traditional pipelines that rely on precise geometric alignment or strict feature engineering, this approach utilizes a Siamese U-Net architecture to learn a generalized metric of signal similarity. 

The model ingests time-synchronized filterbank crops from both the Irish and Swedish stations. Using a U-Net backbone, the architecture preserves the high-fidelity time-frequency structure of the signal during feature extraction. These features are projected into a shared 512-dimensional latent space. By training on a contrastive loss function, the network learns to minimize the Euclidean distance between matched astrophysical signals while maximizing the distance for local RFI. 

This method moves beyond simple pixel-wise cross-correlation; it learns to identify signals as ``matching'' even when they exhibit minor morphological differences due to scintillation or local noise conditions. Preliminary validation, performed using synthetic narrowband and pulsed signals injected into real LOFAR observational backgrounds, demonstrates a distinct bimodal separation in the latent space for signals with Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNR) ranging from 1.5 to 10, offering a robust framework for automated RFI rejection in future survey passes.

\subsection{Sensitivity Benchmarking}

When considering the minimum detectable flux density that an international station can produce, an adapted form of the radiometer equation is used from \cite{pulsar_handbook}. The minimum flux density $(S_{\rm min,chan}, \rm in \ Jy)$ is given as: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
S_{\min,\rm{chan}}(f, l, b) &= \frac{\left[ T_{\text{sky}}(l,b) + T_{\text{int}}(f) \right] \cdot 2 k_B}{A_{\text{eff}}(f)} \cdot \beta \\ & \times \frac{\text{S/N}_{\min}}{\sqrt{n_p\, t_{\text{obs}}\, B_{\text{channel}}}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{W}{P - W}}
 % \label{eq:radiometer_equation}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Here, $f$ is the observing frequency, $T_{\text{sky}}(l, b)$ is the sky temperature at Galactic coordinates $(l, b)$, and $T_{\text{int}}(f)$ is the internal system temperature. $A_{\text{eff}}(f)$ is the effective collecting area of the telescope, $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant, and $\beta$ is a factor accounting for system losses such as digitisation. $n_p$ is the number of summed polarisations, and $B_{\text{channel}}$ is the width of an individual frequency channel. $P$ is the pulsar period and $W$ is the effective-observed pulse width. Each individual channel $S_{\text{min}}$ is calculated with a boolean mask applied to account for RFI contamination, which is taken to be averages found in the band. The collective $S_\text{min,chan}$ for each observation is calculated using quadrature method as shown below:  

\begin{equation}
    S_{\min}(f, l, b) = \left[ \sum_f \frac{1}{S_{\min}(f, l, b)^2} \right]^{-1/2}
    \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{equation}
