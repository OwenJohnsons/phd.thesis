Throughout history humanity has watched the stars and observed transient phenomena. From prehistoric observers sat around campfires catching glimpses of comets and interpreting them as omens, to Chinese astronomers noting the Crab supernovae in 1054. The night sky has always captivated questions from how the Universe works to humanity's place in it. Modern day astronomy at its core is the same, the awe still remains, but the tools have got sharper. With the advent of modern instrumentation the study of transients has expanded from the longest wavelength's to the highest energies.  As a result over the last century new classes of transients have emerged with their own sets of open questions. 

Transients include a large amount of objects that emit across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, it is generally used as an umbrella term to include any astronomical objects that is time-dependent on timescales from nanoseconds to years. This thesis focuses on transients that emit radio waves $(\lambda \approx \text{mm}-\text{km})$.  
Radio waves can be emitted from an astrophysical object by either thermal or non-thermal emission. Any particle above 0~K vibrates when charged particles in atoms undergo acceleration they release electromagnetic radiation. As a consequence of this any particle above 0~K emits thermal radiation through two mechanisms blackbody or bremsstrahlung emission. Alternatively non-thermal emission arises from particles that have non-thermal motion also known as a non-Maxwellian energy distribution. These particles experience external forces that cause motion such as acceleration due to the presence of a magnetic field which commonly produces synchrotron radiation as relativistic electrons spiral around magnetic field lines. 

These emission mechanisms are observed across a wide range of astrophysical phenomena, from objects within Earth’s local neighbourhood such as solar flares, aurorae, and lightning in the outer planets, to some of the most distant and energetic known events including fast radio bursts and gamma-ray bursts. Radio transients span broad ranges of timescales, luminosity, and physical origin. In addition to observed sources, many theoretical radio transients have also been proposed but remain undetected, including primordial black holes, cosmic strings, and, more speculatively, technosignatures from extraterrestrial civilizations.

This chapter aims to non-exhaustively discuss the background and present motivation for studying a set of natural and artificial transients. 

\section{Non-thermal Emission} \label{sec:nonthermal}

Electromagnetic emission can arise in plasmas from both bound atomic systems and unbound particles that are accelerated by external fields. Bound particles have quantized energies and the transition to lower energy states generates photons. However, unbound particles have a continuum of possible energies such that acceleration of these unbound particles can produce a continuum of photon energies. 

For a free (unbound), non-thermal and non-relativistic electron of charge, $e$ and mass $m_e$ that moves in a uniform magnetic field, $\mathbf{B} = \hat z B$. The only Lorentz force present is, $\mathbf{F} = -e(\mathbf{v} \times \textbf{B})$. If this electron is then placed in a circular orbit with a given speed, $v$ and a given radius $r$ the centipedal force causes an acceleration such that, 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{m_ev^2}{r} =evB
\end{equation}

Examining the angular frequency caused by the force balance, $\omega = v/r$. Substituting the following expression is obtained.

\begin{equation}
    \omega_L = \frac{eB}{m_ec} = 2.8~\frac{\text{B}}{\text{G}}~ \text{MHz}
    \label{eq: Larmor Freq}
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq: Larmor Freq} is known as the Larmor frequency, It's significant for setting the fundamental time and frequency scale for how charged particles can gyrate in a magnetic field. However, in an astrophysical context relativistic effects need to be accounted for where $\gamma>1$. With the same setup as before but now with a relativistic electron in a magnetic field, the Lorenz force in this case now takes the form of, 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d p_\perp}{dt} = e \textbf{v}_\perp B
\end{equation}

Where $p_\perp$ is the transverse component of moment, $\textbf{p}_\perp = \gamma m_e \textbf{v}_\perp$. So relativistically the gyro-frequency of a particle is given by the following substitution, $\omega = \textbf{v}_\perp / r$ which results in, 

\begin{equation}
    \omega = \frac{eB}{\gamma m_ec}
    \label{eq:RealtivisticGyroFreq}
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq:RealtivisticGyroFreq} sets the basic orbital timescale for synchrotron-emitting electrons. As the electron becomes relativistic $(\gamma>1)$ the electromagnetic waves are concentrated in a narrow cone of half-angle $\sim1/\gamma$ about the instantaneous velocity vector, $\textbf{v}$, towards the observer which is shown in \cref{fig:SyncotronicEmissionDiagram}.
Or in simpler terms in the electron's rest frame it will just gyrate in a circle at a frequency, $\omega_L$ and be a smooth sinusoid at that frequency. Whereas in the observer's frame, once the electron is relativistic it will be `squashed' due to aberration on a very narrow radiation cone of width, $\sim1/\gamma$ as stated above. The consequence of this is there will only be a single pulse per orbit as a very small fraction of the orbit is pointing towards the observer. For each of these short pulses there will be a spike in electric field as a function of time, with a pulse duration of $\Delta t \sim 1/\gamma^3 \cdot P_{e,\text{orb}}$. The spectrum of radiation of the short pulses is the Fourier transform of the pulse once time and aberration are taken into account. When a short pulse is Fourier transformed it will have power at many frequencies, forming a broad spectrum. So the characteristic frequency of the spectrum is $w_c \sim 1/\Delta t \sim \gamma^3 \omega_L$. This broad spectrum peaks near $\omega_c$, when this is integrated over a power-law distribution of electrons it produces a power-law synchrotron continuum which is observed in many radio transients. 
Pulsar, fast radio bursts, flare stars and long period transients are all examples of non-thermal radio sources. All of which emit coherent radio waves except flare stars which is discussed later in this chapter.

\subsection{Radio Flare Stars} \label{sec:RadioFlareStars}

It has been well documented that our own star, the Sun, and planets in the solar system have radio emission associated with originating from auroras and stellar flares \citep{murphy_lofar_2021, zarka_auroral_1998}. With the advancement of modern radio telescopes, the prospect for capturing radio emission from others and even the prospect of detecting an exoplanet magnetosphere has become achievable \citep{vedantham_coherent_2020}. \

Observing stars especially at low frequency $(\leq 300 \ \text{MHz})$\footnote{$\lambda \sim 1 \ \text{m}$} act as probes into stellar and planetary plasma environments. Coronal Mass Ejections (CMEs) have a low-frequency burst component in which information about the kinematics of plasma can be deduced \citep{villadsen_ultra-wideband_2019}. Incident solar wind is also the primary driving force of auroral emission on magnetized planets. The goal of this project is to explore the viability of detecting radio emission from M and Brown dwarfs using I-LOFAR observations and archive data to put constraints on the prevalence of radio emission from these objects and explore the viability observing radio emission from exoplanets. \

Radio emission from stars is typically produced through CMEs, observed phenomenologically in the Sun as type II and III solar bursts. The radio emission from these events is thought to be produced through the electron-cyclotron maser instability \citep[ECMI;][]{EMI}. The ECMI is a plasma instability that occurs in the presence of a magnetic field and a population of energetic electrons. The instability is thought to be the primary driver of radio emission observed in type II bursts and stars as a whole. With type III bursts being thought to be the result of electron beams that are accelerated in the corona. \

Emission from planets has also been observed in the form of auroral emission. The most notable example of this is the Jovian system. The Jovian system is known to have auroral emission that is driven by the interaction of the solar wind with the magnetosphere of the planet. Coherent radio emission from the aurora is thought to be produced through the cyclotron maser instability \citep[ECM;][]{zarka_auroral_1998} which injects a high-velocity electron population into the magnetosphere. The maximum frequency of the ECM is directly proportional to the magnetic field strength of the object at its emitting point \citep{kavanagh_hunting_2023, joe_nature_review}.

Much time has been spent observing Ultracool Dwarfs ($\text{M7} <$) as they provide a good analogue to the Jovian systems. This allows for direct comparison between the two.  This provides valuable insights into the formation, atmospheric processes, and potential habitability of gas giants like Jupiter. Moreover, such comparative studies contribute significantly to our understanding of planetary evolution and diversity beyond our immediate neighbourhood. Recent detection of radiation belts around a UCD further supports the analogy to Jupiter, as radiation belts are a key characteristic of Jupiter's magnetosphere \citep{joe_nature_review}. \ UCDs, being intermediate in mass between stars and planets, serve as a bridge to understanding exoplanet detection. The discovery of bursting radio emission from a brown dwarf and subsequent detections in UCDs indicate departures from established stellar coronal/flaring relationships. \ Radio bursts from UCDs can exhibit periodic timing \citep{hallinan_rotational_2006} along with strong circular polarization and high brightness temperatures, suggesting the involvement of the ECM process in generating radio emission. Despite a significant amount of gigahertz-frequency radio searches, detection rates for UCD radio emissions remain stubbornly low at around 10\% overall \citep{lynch_radio_2016}.

\section{The Search for Intelligent Life in the Universe}

Arguably one of the most captivating and difficult problems in astronomy is on the question of life elsewhere in the Universe. It's a problem where the known population is statistically insignificant given that Earth is the only known example. However, in the last 50 years evidence has steadily mounted that the conditions for life in the Universe are relativity common. Since exoplanets were first directly detected in 1992 there is (at the time of writing) over 6,300 confirmed exoplanets of all varieties \citep{NEA12}. It is also a popular opinion that most of the stars within the Galaxy and by extent the Universe have orbiting planets. % Even if a minute amount of planets give rise to life there should be 

However, postulating the conditions in which life evolves and lives long enough and becomes advanced enough for interstellar communications is highly difficult and highly speculative. The search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) has likely been around for 100's of years. But early formal discussions arose in the 19th and 20th centuries, specifically around Mars. Nikola Tesla thought about communication with Martians using a wireless electrical system, Giovanni Schiaparelli argued the case for canals on Mars. 

The birth of modern SETI is generally attributed with \cite{CocconiMorrison1959}'s seminal paper on interplanetary radio communication, around 7 months later Project Ozma was carried out by \cite{Drake:1961bv}. Drake used the 85 ft telescope at the Green Bank Observatory (GBO) to search two nearby stars Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani for civilizations broadcasting in radio. Following this Drake hosted a meeting to discuss paths forward for the field, in preparing for the meeting Drake coined an equation to estimate the prevalence of life in the Milky Way, 

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    N = R_* f_p N_e f_l f_i f_c L 
    }
    \label{eq:Drake_equation}
\end{equation}


 In this expression $N$ is the number of communicative civilizations in the Milky Way Galaxy, $R_{*}$ is the rate of star formation per year in the Galaxy, $f_{p}$ is the fraction of stars that have planets around them, $n_{e}$ is the average number of planets that can potentially support life per star that has planets, $f_{l}$ is the fraction of potentially life-supporting planets that actually develop life, $f_{i}$ is the fraction of planets with life that go on to develop intelligent life and $f_{c}$ is the fraction of civilizations that develop a technology that releases detectable signs of their existence into space. \Cref{eq:Drake_equation} is meant more as a thought experiment, rather than an explicit and empirical value as most the values in the equations have debatable definitions and constraints, especially the last 3 factors. Asking members of the radio transient group, based purely on intuition given somewhat known limits for the first two terms yield answers from 0 to 761. The 0 answer includes Earth, alluding that many consider the definition of intelligence to be something outside the current conventional definition\footnote{\url{https://dictionary.apa.org/intelligence}}. Frank Drake's number plate read NEQLSL, which estimated that the number of civilizations in the Galaxy was determined by their ability to have prolonged lifetimes. As an exercise for the reader its recommended before reading further to try and calculate their own number using just their intuition as the result might surprise your own notion of life in the Galaxy. 

 Since the inception of the Drake equation and Project Ozma there has been an increasing effort throughout the decades to conduct SETI\footnote{For a comprehensive history see \cite{Charbonneau2024}}. In the following sections potential signatures for intelligent life, philosophy of carrying out a SETI survey and surveys to-date will be discussed. 

\subsection{What are Technosignatures?}

Two common terms when discussing the search for life in the Universe are biosignatures and technosignatures. The term biosignature has been around in various forms since the 1960's \citep{lederberg_signs_1965,lovelock_physical_1965}, with first being officially used by the NASA Exobiology Program office in ``An Exobiological Strategy for Mars Exploration'' in 1995. A biosignature refers to a biological process where all non-living biological and chemical (abiotic) effects that could cause the process are not possible. Examples of biosignatures are gases that are formed in metabolic processes that could be detected in exoplanet atmospheres, or more locally  microfossils, isotopic signatures, and organic lipids that may be found in hydrothermal vents on Enceladus. 

On the other hand there are technosignatures. ``Technosignature'' was first coined by Jill Tarter in the early 2000s and is loosely defined as a sign of technology that is not a biosignature \citep{wright_recommendations_2018}. Over the years there has been a lot of discussion on a formal definition of what a technosignature is, especially when it comes to deciding what can be considered ``technology''. Deliberation on this topic is complex and requires much care. For the purposes of this work the following definition proposed by \cite{Sheikh2021} is used when discussing technosignatures. This particular definition was created with the aim to provide interdisciplinary wording that could be broadly used. 

\begin{displayquote}
``A technosignature is a physical feature whose presence or abundance is
a result of the presence of a being that has engaged in goal-directed,
intentional manufacture. Here, manufacture is the process of using a
tool (something itself manufactured, or gathered from the environment)
to manipulate a material in order to create a new form, demonstrating
its makers’ formal or informal physical knowledge.''
\end{displayquote}

For more detailed consideration on this topic please see §1.2 of \cite{Sheikh2021}. 

There are many different items that fit this description especially in a terrestrial context as we are surrounded by technosignatures in our everyday life but here the concern is only with technosignatures that originate from beyond Earth. Sticking with the example of the most popular astronomy biosignature, the search for evidence of metabolic processes in exoplanets atmospheres a cognate technosignature would be those caused by atmospheric pollution. Taking Earth as the example atmospheric pollution is  caused by the creation $\text{NO}_2$ in industrial manufacturing, which meets the above definition for a technosignature. If "Earth was trying to detect Earth", modern day telescopes such as \textit{JWST} could detect $\text{NO}_2$ up to 5.9~ly away \citep{sheikh_earth_2025}. This isn't particularly optimal when trying to search the entire Galaxy for technosignatures. The first and most obvious technosignature to search for is artificial radio emission as it implies the existence of a radio transmitter \citep{CocconiMorrison1959} and is the most abudant form of long distance communication on Earth. So much so that these technosignatures are detected everyday on Earth by radio telescope, in the form of radio frequency interference (RFI), uplinks and down links to satellites among many other things to the dismay of the radio astronomer. as will be later shown throughout this thesis. However, a sliver lining is that human made technosignatures can such as satellites like \textit{Voyager 2} can be used as a means to test SETI software and detection pipelines. 

As with most aspects of searching for technosignatures all knowledge hypothesising what a signal might look like follows the assumption that other civilisations will discover the laws of physics and develop technologies like radio transmitters for their own use, similarly to humanity. These signals are compelling for three primary reasons, the first being the relative abundance on Earth, the second being that its more energy efficient to transmit a signal with a smaller fractional bandwidth, i.e. $\Delta f \sim 1~\text{Hz}$ and the second is that they are easy to distinguish from natural signals which appear broadband due to natures random processes $(\Delta f \geq 10^{-3}-10^{-2})$, which in turn makes narrowband radio emission distinctly anthropomorphic. 

Apart from conventional narrowband transmissions there are many other speculative proposed technosignatures. But before outlining some of the interesting examples in the literture it is a reasonable point to introduce the Kardashev scale. The Kardashev scale is often quoted in technosignature literature as a means of benchmarking survey sensitivity and contextualising proposed technological complexity of the technosignature being searched for. Initially developed by \cite{Karadashev_1964} and extended by \cite{Sagan1973CosmicConnection} it outlines three primary types of energy consuming civilisations. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Type I ($10^{16}-10^{17}$ W) civilisations can use all the energy on their home planet. For example, the power of all the incident radiation on the planets surface from the host star. 

    \item Type II ($\sim10^{26}$ W) civilisations can use all the energy outputted by their local star. See below discussion on Dyson Spheres and Swarms. 
    
    \item Type III ($>10^{26}$ W) civilisations can use energy that's on a galactic scale. 
    
\end{enumerate}

The scale itself can be calculated using the following, 

\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{\log_{10} (P) -6}{10}
    \label{eq:KardashevScale}
\end{equation}

Where $P$ is power usage in Watts. The \cite{EI2026StatisticalReview} gave the total global energy supply in 2025 as 500 EJ which on average is 19 TW. Using this in \cref{eq:KardashevScale} gives a value of $K = 0.728$ for Earth. The estimated increase to 2035 of global power used is $8-15\%$, a crude estimate using $P(t) = P_0 (1+r)^t$ where $r$ represents a 2\% increase in energy per. Assuming that energy demands remain on track\footnote{This is a very unlikely case.} year humanity would reach a K value equal to 1 in around the mid-24th century (2340s). \cite{Sagan1973CosmicConnection} also proposed an alphabetical scale to attempt to quantify a civilisations social development by the amount of bits of available information starting at $10^6$ for A and ending with $10^{31}$ bits for Z.  In 1973 Sagan placed humanity at 0.7 H, in 2025 Earth is 0.728 S. Sagan believed that humanity would make first contact with another civilsation somewhere in the range 1.5 J and 1.8 K.

An example of why one might use the Karadshev scale is due the required power consumption. As civilisations develop and require more energy the use of megastructures harnessing a stars energy may be used as a possible solution. A Dyson sphere is one such megastructure which would enclose a star to capture a large fraction of its radiant energy \cite{dyson_search_1960}. Its been pointed out that these would be incredibly difficult to construct with current and envisioned technologies \citep{smith_review_2022,wright_dyson_2020}. An alternative and more viable enginnering alternative is a Dyson swarm which has the same concept but made up of many satellite elements (geostationary constellations) instead of one continuous megastructure \citep{smith_review_2022}. Observationally megastructures may manifest as anomalies in transiting light-curves, anomalous stellar variability or luminosity changes that could be interpreted as controlled energy extraction or stellar engineering, and directed optical, infrared, or X-ray laser pulses that would appear as extremely bright \citep{dyson_search_1960, wright_dyson_2020,suazo_project_2024}. There are a number of instruments and works dedicated to search for these types of technosignatures. For example PANOSETI in a panoramic visible and near-infrared instrument that covers 1,000 square degrees of the sky simultaneously searching for pulsed laser emission. Some other studies have used the vast amounts of light curves Transiting Exoplanet Satellite (\textit{TESS}) as acquired to search for anomalous megastructure light curves and the advent of new large scale observatories such as LSST are covering vast amount of sky at an unprecedented cadence ushering in a new means to do search for technosignatues for an array of technosignatures including megastructures. \subsection{How to detect radio technosignatures}

In this section the means in-which technosignatures are detected will be described. For now, the process by which a radio telescope produces science-usable data is not considered, as it is discussed in detail in \cref{chap:radioastro}. For the purposes of this explanation, radio astronomy data is stored in filterbanks, which are three-dimensional data cubes containing radio spectra as a function of time, known as dynamic spectra.

As mentioned above one of the motivations for searching for narrowband signals is that they are narrow as they originate from engineered oscillators. This means that frequency resolution of a few Hz is required to search for narrowband signals, for example the data used in this thesis uses data that is $\sim 3~\text{Hz}$ in resolution. 

A consequence of searching for such narrow signals is that the relative motion between an observer and the transmitter itself will be, 

\begin{equation}
    f(t) = f_0 +\dot f t. 
\end{equation}

where $f$ is received or emitted frequency, $\dot f$ is the drift rate, 

\begin{equation}
    \dot f = \frac{a_\tau}{c} f. 
\end{equation}

Where $a_\tau$ is the relative acceleration along the line of sight (\si{\metre\per\square\second}). Ordinarly a doppler shift is expressed in terms of velocity, $\Delta f/f = v_r/c$ but as relative velocity changes with time acceleration is used. 

For an extraterrestrial transmitter there are several contributions to $a_\tau$. For the observers on Earth, the planet's rotation causes a Doppler drift over the course of a few hours of about 0.03 \si{\metre\per\square\second}. The Earth also travels around the Sun orbital velocity changes constantly, which contributes around 0.006\si{\metre\per\square\second}. These same contributions come into play for the transmitted signal, where the rotation of their planet and orbital velocity needs to be given some consideration. This contribution is attributed to the standard central acceleration formula. However, a more general expression for this includes instantaneous projected acceleration which also is dependent on the orbital phase of the system. 

% \begin{equation}
%     a_{orb} = \frac{GM_{\text{M}_\odot}}{r^2} \sin i. 
% \end{equation}




\begin{equation}
    a_{orb} = \frac{GM_{\text{M}_\odot}}{r^2} \sin i \cos (\Omega t + \phi). 
    \label{eq:doppler_drift_los_orb}
\end{equation}

Where $r$ is the orbital radius and $i$ in the inclination of the orbital plane with respect to the observer. $\Omega$ is the orbital angular frequency and $\phi$ is orbital phase. The $\cos (\Omega t + \phi)$ ensures that the only component included at a given instant is along the line of sight. Consequently, the measured drift varies throughout the orbit where it's at its max with dealing with systems that are edge on and at a minimum when the system is face on. 

\Cref{eq:doppler_drift_los_orb} indicates that the drift rate will inflate for close in planets with fast rotational periods, binaries with fast orbital period or transmitters around compact objects such as black holes and neutron stars. Also because $\dot f \propto f$ drifting becomes more evident with increased observational frequency. \cite{2019ApJSofiaMaxDriftRate} details the nine terms that contribute to acceleration of a narrowband signal and also calculates the fractional drift-rates $(\dot f/f)$ for an array of planetary systems and compact objects.  Where Earth's contribution is 0.11 nHz and Neutron Star is $6.2 \times 10^{12}$ nHz \citep[see table 2,][]{2019ApJSofiaMaxDriftRate}. \cite{li_developing_2023} extends this work by modelling the drift rate values for $\sim 300$ exoplanets and find that $99\%$ of signals fall within $\pm53~\text{nHz}$, this work also recommends that $\pm44~\text{nHz}$ would be reasonable enough to include $99\%$ of Kepler\footnote{F, G, K, and M type stars} like stars when doing a narrowband search on targets without a confirmed exoplanet. 

The reason for highlighting these values is due to the computational resources it takes to de-drift radio astronomy data. To recover a narrowband signal dynamic spectra must be searched over many trail drift-rates correcting each frequency of each time sample. De-drifting (or de-Doppler) correction aligns the drifting single to a single frequency channel, which maximises the signal-to-noise (analogous to the DM correction discussed in \cref{sec:DispersionMeasure}). 

To implement this one could independently shift and sum the spectra for every single drift rate. For a search that covers $N_f$, frequency channels and $N_t$ time samples for $N_d$ which scales\footnote{This is `Big-O notation' which is commonly used to describe how the running time or memory usage of an algorithm scales as the size of the input data increases. } as $\mathcal{O}(N_f N_tN_d)$. This quickly becomes an issue as modern radio astronomy is data intensive. For example a 15-minute observation using the narrowband data from this thesis would have 27000832 channels at 18 second resolution, combining that with drift rate search equivalent to $\pm53~\text{nHz}$ at 150~MHz would push the computations to over $1\times 10^{12}$.

To try and overcome this limitation \cite{1974A&AS...15..367T} introduced a tree algorithm which was used in pulsar searching. Instead of evaluating every drift path independently it took advantage of the fact that neighbouring drift rate share partial common sums. These intermediate sums can are reused recursively reducing the number of brute force computations by $\mathcal{O}(N_fN_t\log_2N_d)$. Repeating the above calculation brings the number of operations needed to $1\times10^{10}$. This approach forms the basis of modern narrowband search pipelines. 

For example, a widely used package is \textsc{turboSETI} \citep{Enriquez:2017}. Rather than evaluating every possible Doppler drift path independently, turboSETI implements a modified Taylor tree algorithm that recursively combines neighbouring drift trajectories, dramatically reducing the computational cost while producing identical detection statistics to a brute-force search.


\subsection{Surveys to Date}

At the time of writing SETI experiments have been ongoing for more than 60 years. After Frank Drake's initial study in Greenbank there have been many SETI survey's conducted in the following decades\footnote{see \url{https://technosearch.seti.org/radio-list/} for a complete list of published surveys up to 2022.}, some notable examples are Ohio State's "Big Ear" program which detected the "Wow!" signal and SERENDIP programme at Berkeley which used commensal (piggy back) observations to search for technosignatures at Greenbank and later Arecibo. The SERENDIP project is particularly intresting due to the 1000s of hours of observations that were analysed using computational power from millions of users all around the world with through the SETI@home a project that began in 1999 up until 2020.

There is also the Allen Telescope Array (ATA) which was purpose built for SETI purposes in Northern California. The ATA is a 43 dish instrument which has carried out numerous SETI surveys since the mid 2000s and is still actively developed and used today for modern technosignature surveys. One notable survey is the "HabCat" star survey which covered 1-9~GHz with sub‑Hz spectral resolution, and long‑term monitoring of low‑mass stars and the Galactic Centre for narrowband or pulsed technosignatures.

The Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) in Western Australia has been used for several low‑frequency radio SETI campaigns over roughly the last decade, including searches toward known exoplanets and the Galactic Centre at 98–155~MHz. These surveys employ wide fields of view and relatively coarse spectral resolution, significantly extending technosignature coverage into a frequency regime that early SETI largely ignored.

China’s Five‑hundred‑meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) in the last half decade has conducted highly sensitive targeted technosignature observations, notably multi‑beam coincidence‑matching campaigns toward 33 known exoplanet systems in the 1.05–1.45~GHz band. These searches achieve unprecedented sensitivity as FAST is the most sensitive single dish telescope on Earth (see \cref{chap:radioastro}). Fast has also carried out special targets of interest such as the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS. 

Over the past decade there has been a larger amount of technosignature research being outputted. This is in large part due to the Breakthrough Listen (BL) programme. BL is \$100 million dollar initiative that aims to conduct the most extensive radio technosignature search to date. The programme runs surveys on Green Bank, Parkes, MeerKAT, VLA, LOFAR (\cref{sec:SETIchapter}), GMRT, SRT and other facilities. 

One of the primary goals of the work carried out in this thesis was to develop surveys at the lower end of the radio regime. As shown in \cref{fig:chap1SETIfreq} and the aforementioned MWA studies there has been little SETI work done in the sub-200~MHz range in comparison to the rest of the spectrum. \Cref{sec:SETIchapter} and \cref{chap:LOFTS} outline the work that has gone into trying to better fill out technosignature parameter space. 

\subsection{SETI, what is it good for?}

If not already clear at the time of writing there have been no confirmations of a technosigtures, despite a few close calls (see. BLC1, WOW!). SETI in general has long time been critiqued for many reasons, so much so that it was given "Golden Fleece Award" by U.S. Senator William Proxmire in 1978. SETI permanently lost all U.S. based funding in 1993 when congress cancelled all programmes realted to SETI outright. Since then SETI has survived primarily on the donations from private sector philanthropy from individuals like Yuri Milner, Paul Allen and Franklin Antonio to name a few. 

Despite criticisms, speculative and setbacks SETI has been proven to be a worthwhile endeavour for a plethora of reasons. Good SETI surveys or experiments are those that yield beneficial scientific results in the absence of a detection. \cite{sheikh_nine_2020} present the case that all technosignature searches should follow 9-axes of merit. While one could discuss this topic at length, for the purposes of this argument the ancillary science benefits are outlined. Technosignature surveys push radio telescopes to the limit as they require high frequency resolution and advance analysis methods to discern between terrestrial and extra-terrestrial technosignatures. 

As technosignature surveys aim to find a "needle in a cosmic haystack" searches probe regions of parameter space not always covered by conventional surveys, improving general understanding of what is natural and artificial in the radio sky. Technosignature campaigns have driven advances radio  receivers, digital backends, signal processing, RFI‑mitigation techniques, and transient search pipelines that benefit transient astronomy and discovery. A number of major scientific results in other fields of radio astronomy have been made as a result of technosignature research. There is also an argument to be made for the for studying human societies, values, and expectations about humanities place in the cosmos. It has been highlighted that simply conducting SETI introduces conversations and research into thoughts on intelligence, assembly theory, deciding the sociological implication of whether to transmit signals (METI; messaging extraterrestrial intelligence). Interpreting ambiguous signals reveals a slew of information about contemporary cultures (i.e. Earth centric models), political systems, and ethical frameworks. However, discussion on these topics are somewhat out of the scope of this thesis. If the reader has further interest into these topics please see X. 

\subsection{The Cosmic Haystack}

 The final point of discussion on technosignatures is the cosmic haystack. As often echoed in medical and biological research but not as much in astronomy. Null results are still results, so there has been a great deal of thought into how quantify the progress of technosigantures searches. The field as a whole is often thought of finding a needle in the cosmic haystack and its long been agreed upon that one should search the whole haystack for the needle. However, it is difficult to define what exactly the haystack is. Jill Tarter popularised the idea that SETI was akin to filling a glass full of water from the ocean and concluding that there was no fish in the ocean. With the more recent resurgence in technosignature work the glass is more like a bathtub now.  While there is no way to know the extent or parameters of the haystack the three most common ones seen in literture and in this thesis to quantify the progress of a survey on constraining the parameter space is, 

 \begin{enumerate}
     \item Sensitivity, minimum detectable signal from a transmitter at some distance, $d$. 
     \item Star volume, a 3-D spatial volume covering the possible transmitter location as a function of solid angle
     \item Frequency, the frequency and bandwidth the search is being conducted at whether its radio, optical or other. 
 \end{enumerate}

 The next one to consider is time and cadence. How long we look for and how often we repeat looking in a particular direction to catch as signal similar to catching some other once of transient object. Works like \cite{wkl18} extend this framework to an 8-D haystack. (space volume, sensitivity, frequency, bandwidth, polarization, modulation, repetition, and time), here the authors estimated that the total haystack volume is $6.4 \times 10^{116}~\text{m}^5\text{Hz}^2\text{s}\text{W}^{-1}$ and all the major surveys done at the time of that work was $2.4 \times 10^{98}~\text{m}^5\text{Hz}^2\text{s}\text{W}^{-1}$ which is $3.8\times 10^{-19}$\%
