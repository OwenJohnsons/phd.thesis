\chapter{Conclusions} \label{chap:conclusions}

This final chapter aims to summarise the long-winded meanderings of the author who claims it wasn't about finding life in the universe, it was always about the friends we made along the way. The following sections will attempt to summarise the disparate results of this thesis, detailing problems that are solved or partially solved and problems that remain open in lieu of the research carried out. 

\section{Redback Searching}

As its probably clear from this searching for radio transients can be difficult. In most cases advanced hardware and software is required to make a meaningful effort to find some of the most exotic objects in the Universe. One such object is J0523.5-2529 (herein J0523). This object was both interesting as it had the largest companion mass of a redback candidate to date. Confirming radio emission and placing as a confirmed redback would add to at the time of writing 34 known redback systems \citep{koljonen_spidercat_2025}, in addition to adding to a small sample size of known objects J0523's would have the largest mass companion observed to date at $0.85\pm0.08\smass$, with the next largest being J0427.9-6704 with a companion mass of $0.65\pm0.07\smass$ \citep{strader_optical_2019}. This object appeared as `low-hanging fruit' as it showed all strong signs being a conventional redback. 

Radio observations were carried out and searched across 47\% of the orbital period and a large range of observing frequencies (\cref{tab:combined_observations}) using Parkes and GBT. The observations totalled 34.5 hours and were extensively searched for periodic and single burst radio emission with no significant emission noted. 

The absence of emission raises questions about the emission state of J0523.5. Whether the system is in a primarily accretion powered state eclipsing potential radio emission. As radio observations have been carried out from 2017-2022 sufficient time has passed for the state to change as seen with other similar systems which have been noted to change on states on timescales of days to years \citep{strader_optical_2019}. Another possibility is that the radio emission is intrinsically feint and can't be detected, or the beam geometry is unfavourable. 

This raises questions about the fraction of redbacks that are radio-quiet. Whether these redbacks still exhibit a rotation powered state and are intrinsically faint or take longer to transition to this state showing a selection bias to radio emission. It might be the case that at a certain companion mass the accretion state is more stable, and it takes further ablation of the companion before this mass transfer rates changes significantly enough to prompt a change to the sub-luminous or rotation powered state. In the unlikely case that accretion isn't causing eclipsing and thus the lack of radio emission the pseudo-luminosity limits from radio observations indicate that this source is feintier than most known MSPs. It also reinforces the notion that higher energy X-ray and $\gamma$-ray emission from MSPs are largely uncorrelated to that of the radio emission. 

As J0523 was initially flagged as a source in 2014 and has been consistently observed since then it would be beneficial to continuously observe one orbital period every few years to place measure the timescale it took for this unique system to transition states. In doing this it would also provide more evidence for or against the aforementioned points. 

What does this mean for the field as a whole, is this non-detection significant? This non-detection presents that there is an incomplete view of redbacks class, it also shows evidence that there might be a bias agaisnt systems with more massive companions. If these higher mass companions are preferentially associated with long‑lived accretion or more opaque winds, then the observed companion‑mass distribution particular the high mass end of this distribution is under‑representing such systems, and evolutionary models built on radio‑confirmed samples will need to account for this incompleteness.

More broadly the case of J0523 could be an example system to strengthen ties between redbacks and other classes of MSPs. Its combination of bright $\gamma$‑ray emission, strong X‑ray and optical variability but the radio non‑detection makes it a potential analogue  to radio‑quiet $\gamma$‑ray pulsars, where the spin‑powered neutron star is clearly active, but the radio beam is either intrinsically faint or obscured along our line of sight. At the same time, its multi‑wavelength behaviour and possible long‑lived accretion or intra‑binary material link it to low‑mass X‑ray binaries and transitional millisecond pulsars like PSR J1023+0038 and XSS J12270–4859, which switch between accretion‑powered LMXB states and radio MSP states on year‑scale timescales. This could all help to unify these populations under a common picture of pulsar‑wind/companion interaction, state transitions, and multi‑wavelength selection effects.

However, the above claims are speculative and to fully push forward the field the parameter space needs to be continual expanded upon with observations of both Redback and Black widow candidates along with continued observations of J0523 over the coming years and potentially with next generation facilities like SKA-Low. 


\section{Crab Giant Pulses}

This thesis tended to analyse large quantities of data constraining various parameters spaces in the pursuit of novel radio emission from beyond Earth natural or otherwise. \Cref{chap:crabpulsar} outlined the alternative, an analysis of a large amount of data of a known target. The Crab itself is extraordinary object in its own right, exceptional bright and young and visible across the entire electromagnetic spectrum one could spend an entire career just on the object itself. 

Over the past half of a decade there has been a large amount of observation time dedicated to observing the Crab with I-LOFAR. This amounted to almost 350 hours over the course of 6 years. The analysis in \cref{chap:crabpulsar} details the processing these observations and mining them for pulses. Over 1,000,000 `regular' pulses and over 500,000 giant pulses (GPs) were collated. This makes it the largest collection of GPs from the Crab to date. The collected GPs that are over 100~S/N were then individually analysed modelling and measuring the scattering and DM across each of the 174 epochs. These values were then used to infer changes in the local nebula.

While the processing and analysis carried out in the \cref{chap:crabpulsar} in a complete body of work, the dataset collected over the last half decade could be used to form basis of a thesis in its own right. For example the next thing one could to do is to use something like \textsc{simpulse} to conduct an in-depth analysis of the limits DM precision that could be determined from individual GPs, pushing the low frequency dataset to its limit. Another project could be a detailed study on the relationship between the scattering and DM, with full statistical treatment of the inferred measurements. With higher cadence observations this relationship could investigate any correlations, if any at shorter timescales \citep{kuzmin_correlation_2008}. Work of this nature could be used to further constrain the size and density of nebular scattering screens, and provide a benchmark for analogous studies of FRBs and their hosts. 

Additonal observations would also refine the low‑frequency power‑law index for main pulse and inter-pulse distributions. Another point of investigation could be to track how the GP flux distribution changes with scattering conditions and the state of the nebula, which also lends itself to linking Crab‑like GPs to the low‑luminosity end of repeating FRBs \citep{sokolowski_100_2025}. One could also conduct an analysis of the flux distribution as a function of frequency.  

For the wider field, a half-decade, low‑frequency Crab dataset with per‑epoch DM and scattering measurements is a valuable resource for understanding how local environments imprint themselves on pulsar signals. Directly complimenting high‑frequency single‑pulse DM work by showing how the nebula and ISM modulate pulse shapes and arrival times at the frequencies where scattering is strongest which is aslo of interest to the FRB community. 

The Crab and pulsars in general remain enigmatic in both their emission physics. It is still uncertain what causes GPs to be so exceedingly bright compared to the regular pulses. I-LOFAR instead does a great job at measuring the consequences that surrounding environment has on these pulses. Future work on this data along with some additional observations might develop stringent methods to separate nebular scattering, intrinsic pulse evolution, and effects of the ISM on an epoch-to-epoch basis. For now much about the detailed 3D structure and time evolution of the scattering screen in the Crabs local neighbourhood remain open.

\section{Dual-site Technosignature Searches}

Before this PhD there had never been research carried out in Ireland where contributing to the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. This thesis marks the first PhD in Ireland where a significant portion of it is based on technosignature science. \Cref{sec:SETIchapter} details the largest Galactic survey to be carried out to date and the largest overall at low frequencies. 

The survey itself was carried out at 110-190~MHz with observations of 1,631,198 targets from \textit{TESS} and \textit{Gaia}. Observations took place simultaneously with two international stations (noninterferometric) of LOFAR based in Ireland and Sweden. The survey was able to reject the presence of any Doppler drifting narrowband  transmissions in the barycentric frame of reference, with equivalent isotropic radiated power of $10^{17}$ W, for 0.4 million (or 1.3 million) stellar systems at 110 (or 190) MHz. This work demonstrates the effectiveness of using multisite simultaneous observations for rejecting anthropogenic signals in the search for technosignatures. 

Before this project there were few observations at below 1~GHz and while initial studies prompted the 1.42~GHz `watering hole' in the early days of technosignature science. The Murchison Widefield Array observed 136 objects \citep{Tingay_2016,Tingay_2018,tingay:omm,tremblay_seti_2020}. Keeping in mind the arguments for searching the entire haystack across the entire frequency domain and the availability of two identical LOFAR stations to test coincidence rejection as a proof of concept. 

While the survey was a successful piolet survey and showed much potential as with any survey that returns a null result the next step is to think of ways to improve it and in keeping with SETI's axes of merit for surveys ensuring ancillary science goals was a priority for the next rendition. It was agreed that the next implementation of a LOFAR technosignature survey would either have to be a ten times more sensitive or ten times more observations, which is where the idea for LOFTS stemmed. 

\section{A LOw Frequency pulsar, FRB and Technosignature Survey}

As mentioned LOFTS is the successor to the first LOFAR survey with aims to further develop the survey in all aspects. The opportunity to conduct this survey came about during the LOFAR 2.0 upgrade which gave each of the international station directors the discretion to run independent observing campaign. LOFTS was awarded 12/24 hours a week for as long as the stations were operating in local mode. Thus, the criteria of increasing the observing ten times more was met. 

One of the primary goals of LOFTS was to show that technosignature observations can be viewed more than a niche activity, instead they can be naturally integrated into broader time-domain observing programmes. The LOFTS survey was designed based on maximising a range of science goals such as the increased observation time and the near zenith pointings. At the time of writing the full results and ultimately lessons from LOFTS have yet to come to fruition. Technosignature analysis for 30\% of the survey has been complete for a total of \nref \textit{Gaia} sources with the estimated total sourced in the full LOFTS dataset at the time of writing estimated to be \nref. 

\Cref{sec:SETIchapter} also highlighted some important challenges that need to be resolved. It has time and again been shown throughout this thesis that distinguishing rare astrophysical events from terrestrial interference remains difficult, particularly as survey volumes scale up and RFI sources become evermore prevalent on the ground and in the sky. Preliminary machine-learning frameworks developed here demonstrate considerable promise, but require extensive validation on larger datasets before they can replace conventional detection pipelines.

Ultimately the work of a technosignature survey is to detect a technosignature, until this happens (if it happens) the need for continued observations over larger sky areas and longer time baselines are the primary drivers for pushing the field forward. However, as shown in this thesis increasingly capable survey instrumentation and intelligent automated analysis techniques help with this goal. 

The plans for LOFTS at present is to finish processing the entire dataset for both technosignatures and transients and publish this paper as updated and new limits in the 110-190~MHz range for three types of technosignatures as already mentioned. There is also further work in prep detailing the machine learning efforts taking place using the LOFTS data along with the benchmarking of searching for narrowband signals using Lorentzian functions. There is also a slew of coordinated observations that have taken place during this duration of this thesis on interesting targets such as Oumuamua and 4I/ATLAS that will be detailed in works by the wider Breakthrough Listen collaboration. 

\section{Radio Astronomy from the Lunar Farside}

\Cref{chap:lunar} demonstrates the scientific case for observing radio transients from the lunar farside. In this chapter in-depth estimations are made into the type of transient science that can be done from the lunar farside while remaining modest about expectations. In this multiple novel speculations are made about what new transients and parameter space may be probed from the lunar farside. 

The low-frequency transient population below 50 MHz remains largely unconstrained, making it difficult to predict the value of science that could be garnered from observing from the lunar farside. Likewise, the occurrence rates of low-DM Galactic bursts, long-period transients and other low-DM  radio transients counterparts remains somewhat uncertain due to the prevalence of RFI makes especially at higher frequencies. If such a subpopulation exists LFT3 will be well place to observe such objects. Full scientific value does not completely lie with individual detections but in establishing the first statistically meaningful constraints on transient populations within an observational regime that has remained largely inaccessible.

Ultimately, this work shows that the principal scientific value of the lunar farside is not simply that it provides a quieter location for radio astronomy, but that it enables entirely new classes of observations whose feasibility can now be assessed quantitatively. By identifying both the opportunities and the remaining limitations, this chapter establishes a roadmap for the next generation of lunar radio observatories.

\section{Other Works}

\Cref{chap:otherwork} discusses numerous other side quests that were completed during this PhD whether it be as a lead or co-author. A lot of this work was, like much of this thesis carried out through I-LOFAR, either supporting other PI's process their data and developing scripts to aid in further analysis of pulsars for purposes of pulsar timing or characterizing the instrument itself. Plenty of time was also spent on the maintenance of backends, keeping both the software and hardware operating on two upright toothpicks. 

Some other work was carried out in commissioning two GReX units in Birr, Offaly and Hat Creek, Northern California, the former of which was installed on a rainy and muddy evening three days before Christmas. These units continue to observe as part of the GReX units and will hopefully continue to improve constraints on FRBs within the Galaxy further. If lucky, a few Galactic FRBs themselves will be caught further improving the understanding of the origin of these fascinating objects. 

Staying in Birr, but moving a couple meters to the left some similar work is detailed on the using the VLF antenna a global network. Inspired by the distributed observing concept developed for GReX, archival VLF data are being investigated as a global sensing network capable of detecting ionospheric disturbances produced by exceptionally bright GRBs. By exploiting the ionosphere as a giant detector, this work explores the feasibility of estimating the occurrence rate of ultra-bright events, such as GRB 221009A (the BOAT), from their global ionospheric response rather than through direct high-energy observations alone.

Observations of MTP0015 and MTP044 are mentioned in this chapter. Throughout this PhD many similar observations were made on various targets that were thought to have potential low frequency but for the fear of having too many null results and constrained parameter spaces in a single thesis they have been omitted. 

Finally, an overview of the work carried out during a stint at the SETI Institute and NASA is detailed. The work on \textit{Curiosity} aimed to develop computational economically machine learning models that could identify unique and interesting data from mass spectrometers, so less data needed to be sent back to Earth putting further strain on an already at capacity deep space network. The aim of this work was never to deploy it on \textit{Curiosity} but use it as a test bed for future outer solar system missions such as \textit{Dragonfly} that will be at much greater distance with a much higher resolution mass spectrometer, but the bandwidth one expects in the outer solar system. The models developed proved to be successful in accomplishing this while remaining computationally economic, this resulted in the patent of the software by NASA and a successful proof of concept. The project was to be developed to higher technology readiness level but due to current constraints on funding efforts have been placed on hold.

\section{Final Thoughts on Life in the Universe}

This section details some personal subjective thoughts I (the author) have on life in the universe, not that anyone asked. I've been fortunate enough to spend half a decade working on radio astronomy and a good portion of that time has been in the pursuit of finding life elsewhere in the Universe. While I don't personally believe that a technosignature discovery is going to happen within my lifetime, I think it's important to conduct the research anyway. I'd be willing to bet a cup of coffee on it that because of SETI researchers around the world we're a lot closer to that goal than we were yesterday. The ancillary benefits of SETI have already been discussed in depth, when you look for novel things you often find novel things, even if they are not what you expect. I think it's important to acknowledge that humans have an innate desire for connection, there is no getting around it, we are social. I think that's why as an astronomer if you are approached by a member of the public their first question isn't "hey dude, what's up with the emission mechanics of pulsars?" it's usually "Is there life out there other than us?", "Where are they?", "What do you think they look like?". To which I usually answer "Yes", "not a notion" and "I've no idea, but definitely not like us, I'd be quite surprised if aliens are bipedal". I think it humanities distinct nature for connection coupled with a thirst for exploration is what makes this field so fascinating, the introspection that SETI induces is quite remarkable. It brings up questions like "what is intelligence", "where is our place in the stars?" and "where do we go from here". For me personally, SETI makes me think about the way we treat each other and the past four years I've been privileged to travel the world, meet so many wonderful people doing and sit safely in observatories looking for aliens. During those four years I've also read endless news articles about needless conflict and destruction throughout the world. In those four years I've searched millions of stars in our local neighbourhood and found no shred of evidence for life in outside Earth. If I was a champion for anything, if I have one final thought, It's that we could go millions of light years without encountering anyone other living being, it really isn't that hard to be kinder to each other, because as it stand we're all we got.
