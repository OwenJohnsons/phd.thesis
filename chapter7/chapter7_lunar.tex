\section{Introduction}

The lunar far side has long been noted for its unique observing potential by radio astronomers with numerous missions either launched, built or planned \citep[e.g.][]{hibbard_results_2026, goel_probing_2022, brinkerink_dark_2025, bale_lusee_2023, burns_farview_nodate}. Seeing as the Moon is in a tidally locked orbit, it is uniquely positioned as the only place in the solar system that is never in Earth's direct line of sight. This is important as it is thus shielded from any RFI that is being emitted from the Earth's surface and satellites in low orbit. With the RFI environment for ground radio telescopes getting progressively worse \citep[e.g.][]{grigg_growing_2025,LOFAR-Starlink}, the motivation for putting radio instruments on the lunar far side has increased. However, this relatively quiet environment will get vastly `louder' in the coming years.

\section{Science Cases}

The lunar farside offers a unique platform for studying and potentially detecting transient phenomenon. Unprecedented radio frequency interference isolation, unhindered opportunity to monitor during 14-day lunar nights, wide-field multibeam capability, and coverage of frequency bands inaccessible or severely inhibited from Earth. Five primary transient categories drive the science case: pulsars spanning millisecond to ultra-long periods, the enigmatic long-period transients that challenge conventional compact object models, Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) requiring multiband spectroscopy to decode their emission physics, low-dispersion measure transients that probe the local neighbourhood, and solar system emission. This chapter examines three science cases, searching for unknown transients, low-DM transients and Fast Radio Bursts. Individual memos on the aforementioned sources that are mentioned but not covered are available on \url{lft3.space}.

\subsection{Blind Survey for Unknown Transients}

The low-frequency radio sky, particularly 1--100~MHz, is one of astronomy's least probed ranges of the electromagnetic spectrum. Historically, this frequency range has been underexplored due to systematic and observational challenges. Primarily, the ionosphere reflects radio waves below $\sim 10~ \rm MHz$ and highly distorts radio waves up to $\sim 30~\rm MHz$. It is only in exceptional cases where the radio emission is very bright that it can be detected around the 30~MHz threshold \cite{zakharenko_detection_2013, zawdie_calculating_2017}. The global use of the FM band for radio stations also makes it infeasible to observe from Earth, eliminating a unique bandwidth that could otherwise be searched and used in wideband studies of transients. 
Far side lunar observatories such as LFT3 present the first opportunities to map the sky and discover a potential unknown class of low-frequency transients. \\ 

Historically, there have been surveys that have attempted to do a blind search for transients at very low frequencies. One recent survey of note was the LOFAR Multi-frequency Snapshot Sky Survey which looked near the North Celestial Pole for 35 hours at 60~MHz covering 175~deg$^2$, which found one astrophysical candidate \citep{stewart_lofar_2016}. This single detection implies the presence of a potentially new class of transients that is difficult to observe for a multitude of reasons from ground observatories. \\
The Long Wavelength Demonstrator Array's all-sky survey at 73.8~MHz detected zero transients above a threshold of 500~Jy \citep{lazio_surveying_2010}. Similar surveys with the Murchison Widefield Array at 154~MHz also yielded similar null detections to a level of 5.5~Jy \citep{bell_survey_2014}. Yet sporadic detection of $\sim$kJy level events have been detected by the Long Wavelength Array's all-sky monitor \citep{obenberger_limits_2014} demonstrates that there is a population of bright transients that do exist, they just emit on rarer timescales to that of the cadence of sub 100~MHz frequency surveys. The success of projects like the {LOFAR} {Tied}-{Array} {All}-{Sky} {Survey} ({LOTAAS}) which discovered 100's of transients from 110--190~MHz further indicating the presence of transients at low frequencies.

Due to the large amount of scattering that is present at lower frequencies, as demonstrated in \cref{chap:crabpulsar} and shown in \cref{fig:DDplan_lunar} finding fast transients becomes increasingly difficult as frequency decreases. In recent years, the discovery of long period transients has opened up a new parameter space to explore. The 2022 discovery of GLEAM-X J0704-37 \citep{hurley-walker_radio_2022} and subsequent similar objects \citep{bloot_strongly_2025, men_highly_2025, wang_detection_2025} were all detected within LFT3 observational bandwidth with varying fluxes. Preliminary calculations (see \cref{fig:sens_grids}) indicate that there will be a visible population from the lunar-far-side. Due longer burst durations (few seconds to minuetes) than that of typical pulsar, smearing at low frequencies and loss in signal-to-noise from DM mismatch will be a lot less significant (see \cref{fig:DDplan_lunar} and \cref{fig:DM-Trial}) and some cases neglible.  



These sources challenge conventional pulsar physics: standard models predict that neutron stars cease radio emission once rotational energy loss drops below thresholds for magnetospheric pair production, typically at spin periods exceeding 10–20 seconds. Yet, periods of LPTs are that of minutes to hours, occupying a parameter space that is forbidden by canonical pulsar models \cite{pulsar_handbook}. At present, there are many proposed models to include these objects within current theoretical framework. One proposed idea is that these objects are isolated magnetars with magnetic fields in excess of $10^{14}~\rm G$ \cite{men_highly_2025}. Alternatively, magnetic white dwarf binary systems could natural produce periods on this timescale to match their orbital periods, with radio emission powered by accretion streams rather than rotation. The discovery that several long-period transients possess red dwarf companions and exhibit spectral energy distributions peaking at optical wavelengths strengthens the white dwarf interpretation for at least a subset of the population \cite{de_ruiter_sporadic_2025, pelisoli_constraints_2025}. \\ 

Another interesting population for LFT3 to observe are Galactic magnetars. At the time of writing there are around 30 of these objects confirmed with 6 of them exhibiting radio emission. The 20\% emitting fraction more than likely reflects observation bias in oppose to some intrinsic properties of magnetars, where most are discovered through higher energy X-ray and $\gamma$-ray emission. There is a proposed subset of `old' magnetars that are in excess of $10^{5}$ years old, where X-ray luminosity has greatly diminished below detection threshold but still may be good candidates for radio emission. \\ 

In April 2020 there was a detection of an FRB-like burst from a Galactic magnetar, SGR 1935+2154. This burst had a fluence of 2.5~MJy~ms at 1.4~GHz, making it extraordinarily bright for a Galactic source and of energies typical of FRBs if the source was placed at an extragalactic distance. Subsequent radio observations of magnetar XTE J1810-197 revealed lower-energy radio bursts spanning five to six orders of magnitude below FRB 20200428D, suggesting a continuum of magnetar burst energies, with only the brightest examples detectable at extragalactic distances. Any detection of one of these objects from the lunar farside would be of significant scientific value. \\ 

A blind survey from the lunar farside uniquely addresses this population, with the possibility for continuous 14 day observations at various integrations and cadences. Previous surveys would miss most of these sources, as they would need at least 10 hours of regular observations. LFT3 has the potential to take snapshot observations, as the lunar night's uninterrupted monitoring eliminates this selection bias entirely. 

A common figure of merit of transient surveys is the speed to which they can be conducted, which is also important to consider in the context of lunar based missions that have significantly smaller operational lifespans and sensitivitives than ground based observatories. Survey speed scales with as follows, $(A_e/T_{\rm sys})^2 \cdot \rm FoV$, where $A_e$ is the effective area and $T_{\rm sys}$ is the system temperature of the instrument. The resulting value is proportional to the rate which an instrument can cover independent sky positions to a fixed flux density (i.e. more sky to that depth per unit observing time). The use of the Phased Array Feed (PAF) on LFT3, which operates from 300 -- 3000~MHz with 10 beams that have a field of view of $4-60^\circ$ . This equates to survey speed values of $3.67 \times 10^{-1}$ to $2.02 \ \rm (m^2/K)^2 \ deg^2$ for LFT3's PAF, each of the instruments and their survey speed are illustrated in \cref{fig:sensitivity_plot}




\subsection{Searching for low-DM Transients}

One of the most potentially fruitful transient science goals of LFT3 could come from searching for low-DM transients. As the lunar farside will be a realtiviley pristine environment (see Ashe et al. 2026) searching for low-DM transients is feasible. In the context of LFT3 `low-dm' events are considered to be $\lesssim10~\text{pc cm}^{-3}$. Low dispersion measures correspond to very modest dispersive delays across the observing band and path lengths of at most a few hundred parsecs through the ISM. In other words, LFT3 will be able to probe the local neighbourhood \footnote{517~pc when looking at Galactic Centre \citep{ocker_ne2025_2026}} for transients. Specifically nearby magnetars and neutron stars, possible white-dwarf binaries, and any FRB-like bursts within the solar neighbourhood. \\ 

Observing low-DM's from the ground has been difficult to impossible depending on the observing frequency. One of the primary reasons for this is due to the presence of RFI making low-DM, high fluence bursts easier to confuse with RFI. In most survey cases, candidates at low-DM's are treated as interference by default. Couple this with the
LFT3's unprecedented radio environment eliminates this constraint. No longer forced vetoing low-DM candidates a priori, and can instead design the search specifically around this parameter space. \\ 

There are several outstanding questions already in the field that discovery of low DM transients at LFT3's highest frequencies could help answer. Counting FRB200428 and any similar detections the prevalence magnetars and how often they produce bright radio bursts could be constrained, using population models to infer a per-magnetar burst rate.  FRB~200428 has already demonstrated that at least some FRB-like bursts come from magnetars in the Milky Way at $\sim$GHz frequencies along with sources like XTE J1810-197 as previously mentioned.  Any nearby FRB-like bursts with $\mathrm{DM}  \lesssim 10~\text{pc cm}^{-3}$ would provide uniquely bright, local benchmarks for testing FRB emission models and for tying together the Galactic and extragalactic FRB populations. A low-DM sample that lacks obvious magnetar counterparts would directly probe alternative Galactic progenitors, such as old, radio-loud magnetars that are X-ray quiet, RRAT-like neutron stars, or compact white-dwarf systems.\\

Operationally, LFT3 can implement a low-DM search on most science data products as outlined in Prabu et al. 2025 across its entire bandwidth. However, observing frequency will place a constraint on the range of pulse-widths that will be detectable especially at the lower part of the band where smearing is dominant, this is discussed in detail in \cref{sec:transientSpecs}. Any low-DM candidates that are present in LFT3's data will be analysed and treated as a separate class in the search pipeline and analysis. Coincidence across multiple beams of the phased array, and characteristic pulse morphologies can be used to confirm a sources' nature from RFI or instrumental effects.\\

Over the nominal mission lifetime, even a few even one low-DM detections would have disproportionate impact. One or more FRB~200428-like bursts from Galactic magnetars would directly calibrate the Galactic FRB-like event rate and test whether such events are rare curiosities or a generic phase of magnetar evolution. A sample of very local low-DM bursts, or a stringent upper limit on their rate, would either reveal or tightly constrain the population of radio-loud but high-energy–quiet magnetars and other nearby compact objects. In all cases, the low-DM search naturally complements LFT3's broader wideband science programme. Nearby, high signal-to-noise events provide the local benchmarks against which the properties of the much larger cosmological FRB population can be interpreted.

\subsection{Observations of FRBs}

Taking the present population of extragalactic FRBs known from the CHIME catalog \citep{collaboration_first_2021} the science case for LFT3 is limited. The main constraint for FRB science from the lunar far-side lies more with the pulse broadening effects seen at extragalactic distances. Here scattering  along the line of sight to LFT3 from an FRB will dwarf the emission duration and cause too much smearing to be detectable \citep{bhat_clean-based_2003}. This effect limits observations of FRBs based on observing frequency and burst duration. This scattering, coupled with the typically steep sensitivity requirements imposed by the resulting pulse broadening, implies that no currently known extragalactic FRB in the CHIME population would be detectable. 

This parameter space remains as a reasonable science goal as it is no extra cost to attain the dataproducts. An extragalactic detection of an FRB in the LFT3 band would imply an intrinsically bright burst with interesting scattering properties that if seen across a large fraction of the observing band would provide valuable science. Conversely, continued non-detections would place meaningful limits on the low-frequency transparency of FRB emission and help distinguish between intrinsic spectral turnover and propagation-induced suppression.

While the present known Galactic population is inaccessible as mentioned there is an analogous population of FRBs from Galactic magnetars that remain viable targets, like FRB 20200428. there have been several initiatives to try and detect this population such as STARE \citep{bochenek_stare2_2020}, which did detect 20200428 and more recently GReX \citep{shila_grex_2025}. LFT3 would be a more sensitive version of these instruments coupled with a better observing environment and large bandwidth on a large proportion of the sky.

\subsection{De-dispersion}

As discussed extensively in \cref{sec:DispersionMeasure} Dispersion Measure (DM) causes a time delay in the arrival of the radio wave based on its frequency, which can be expressed as $\Delta t = \text{DM}/(\mathcal{D}f^2)$. Where $\mathcal{D \simeq}~2.41\times10^{-4}~\text{MHz}^{-2}$ known as the dispersion constant and $f$ is the observing frequency \cite{kulkarni_dispersion_2020}. 

For fast transients (FRBs, LPTs, RRATs and Pulsars) the data must be de-dispersed to account for pulse broadening that occurs due to propagation through the ISM caused by DM. Without these corrections, pulses would be smeared, resulting in a loss of signal-to-noise and in most cases a non-detection of a detectable pulse. The contributing effects up to a DM of 50 \dm are shown in \cref{fig:DDplan_lunar}. In this figure, the inherent factors from de-dispersion and the scattering contributions are shown as a function of DM. Contributions from intra-channel smearing and scattering are also functions of frequency as well as DM. Here, the most severe values of smearing are taken, which is the lowest frequency channel. Incoherent de-dispersion corrects dispersion after channelisation and thus leaves residual intra-channel smearing, whereas coherent de-dispersion removes dispersion in the voltage domain and can in principle recover the intrinsic pulse shape. However, coherent de-dispersion can only be done for transients of a known DM as coherent de-dispersion across an entire band is computationally infeasible. For a more in-depth discussion see appendix \ref{appen:low-frequency-dd}.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

The detectability of low‑frequency radio sources depends jointly on the structure of the Galactic radio background and the intrinsic performance of the receiving system. At the frequencies relevant to LFT3, both the sky temperature and the instrumental response vary strongly with frequency and sky position, so it is essential to model the frequency–dependent sky brightness and the corresponding instrument sensitivity in a consistent framework before interpreting survey yields.

\subsubsection{Sky Temperature}
For frequencies that are greater than 10~MHz, the centre of the Galactic plane is brighter than nearer the poles \citep{manning_galactic_2001}. This occurs because synchrotron emission from relativistic electrons in the Galactic magnetic field dominates at these frequencies, concentrating brightness along the denser plane and toward the centre. As the observing frequency decreases towards a few megahertz, free-free absorption by the warm ionized medium increasingly suppresses emission along longer sight lines through the disk.  

This variation disappears around 5~MHz, becoming more equivocal, then at ultra low frequencies around a MHz the opposite is true, the poles are brighter to oppose to the centre of the plane. Here, the competing effects of synchrotron emission and absorption produce a nearly isotropic sky, with minimal contrast between plane and poles. The transition reflects the optical depth through the Galactic disk approaching unity. 

At these wavelengths, free-free optical depth becomes so high that the inner plane is heavily absorbed, inverting the high-frequency pattern and making high-latitude regions appear brighter. This reversal is key for interpreting low-frequency radio observations. 

Surveys of the sky temperature have been limited on Earth as previously mentioned. LFT3 would provide one of the first modern opportunities to obtain all-sky measurements at 30~MHz and below. By mapping the frequency at which the Galactic plane transitions from emission-dominated to absorption-dominated, LFT3 could place direct constraints on the distribution of thermal electrons, the free-free optical depth of the warm ionized medium, and the large-scale structure of the Galactic magnetic field.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}

The lunar farside provides an observing environment that is fundamentally different from any location accessible to ground-based radio astronomy. Shielding from terrestrial radio frequency interference, the absence of an ionosphere, and uninterrupted observing during the two-week lunar night collectively enable regions of observational parameter space that have remained inaccessible from Earth. As the lunar environment becomes increasingly occupied by scientific and commercial infrastructure, LFT3 represents a timely opportunity to exploit this exceptionally radio-quiet environment before it is permanently altered.

This chapter has demonstrated that, although LFT3 will not compete with large terrestrial facilities in raw collecting area, its unique observing location enables science that cannot be replicated elsewhere. The combination of wide instantaneous bandwidth, multiple observing bands spanning 0.1--2700~MHz, and phased array beamforming provides the capability to undertake blind transient surveys, search for nearby low-dispersion measure events, and perform simultaneous broadband observations over frequencies that are either inaccessible or severely contaminated from Earth.

Overall, these novel transient science cases coupled with the other science cases proposed for the mission \citep{DeBoer2026LFT3MNRASS} demonstrate that LFT3 occupies a unique niche among proposed lunar missions. Rather than replacing existing ground-based facilities, it complements them by extending observations into frequency bands and radio environments that cannot be accessed from Earth. The mission potentially provides an opportunity to study known classes of transient sources under entirely new observing conditions, but also probe previously unexplored parameter space for radio transients.
